# The Spare He Couldn't Convert

The pinsetter on Lane 7 didn't cycle. It exhaled. A pneumatic sigh, then the distinct *thwock-thwock-thwock* of wood striking wood, ten pins resetting themselves in the dark without a ball thrown.

Marta didn't look up from her crossword. "Lane 7," she said into the headset. "Ghost game. Again."

"Again?" Dennis crackled through the speaker, chewing. "Third this week. Pull the relay?"

"Pull it and the league bowlers complain the machine's 'cold.' Leave it and they complain it's 'haunted.' Corporate has no code for haunted."

She capped her pen. The alley smelled of lane oil, fried dough, and rental-shoe dust. Through the smoked glass, thirty-two lanes slept under fluorescent hum. Only Lane 7 glowed, its monitor cycling a name: *HAROLD - GAME 1 - FRAME 10.*

The side door banged. Cold rain air cut the oil.

"Marta." Her father. Sixty-eight, shoulders narrowed by a stroke, jacket two sizes large from Goodwill. He carried a bowling bag—her mother's old tote. "The league secretary said you're working. Said the Vikings need a sub tonight. Make-up match. Pin Pals."

"The Vikings dropped you in '98. You quit. Postcard from Reno: 'Chasing the 300.' We sold your ball to a sitcom prop truck."

"I'm back. Doctor cleared me. Mostly." He tapped the tote.

The pinsetter *thwocked*. A strike flash bloomed for *HAROLD*. Frame 10, Ball 1. X.

Marta stared at the screen. "Harold. Your league name."

"Was."

"You haven't bowled in twenty-six years. Your number's purged. You're a ghost in the system."

"I'm a sub. Rules allow subs."

"Rules require a sanctioned average, a current card, a signed waiver. You have a stroke hand and a tote."

"I got a ride home if I bowl good. If not... bus pass."

The monitor blinked: *HAROLD - FRAME 10 - BALL 2 - WAITING.*

Dennis: "Marta? Kill power to 7?"

The league bowlers arrived in forty minutes. The Pin Pals. The Vikings—four men Marta had watched age through three divorces, two heart attacks, one transition. They bowled for the prize fund, the banana splits, the fragile architecture of Tuesday night.

Her father stood by the ball return. The tote sat between them like a contract.

Marta reached for the keyboard. *ADD SUBSTITUTE. ENTER NAME. ENTER AVERAGE.*

She typed: *HAROLD. 142.* A lie. His real average, twenty-six years dead, was 198. But 142 was the house minimum for a blind score. It kept the team legal. It kept the match official. It kept the money in the fund.

"Lane 7," she said. "Open for practice. One game only."

Her father smiled, lopsided and wet. He unzipped the tote.

"Dad. If you gutter the first frame, I'm putting the bumpers up. Bylaws be damned."

"I wouldn't dream of it, Marta." He pulled out the ball—polished black, finger holes worn smooth. "I'm picking up the spare."

The pinsetter *thwocked*. The monitor advanced: *HAROLD - FRAME 1 - BALL 1.*

She watched him walk to the approach. The lights caught the oil pattern, a geography only they could read.
