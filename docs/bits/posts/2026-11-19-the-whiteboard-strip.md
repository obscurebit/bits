# The Whiteboard Strip

Row 12. Mrs. Alvarez. Dialysis. Tuesday. Thursday. Saturday. 5:15 AM pickup. The van idles in the cul-de-sac while her grandson argues with the driver about the wheelchair ramp angle. I type the time into the shared spreadsheet. The dispatch software refuses the import. Column F stays red. I delete the text from Driver Mike on my personal phone: *She’s not ready. Again.* The laminated whiteboard strip on the filing cabinet smears when I lean my elbow on it. *ALVAREZ — 0515 — EXCEPT* written in black dry-erase. The marker tip is frayed. I rewrite it. The smear becomes a shadow.

Row 13. Mr. Chen. Wound clinic. Monday. Wednesday. Friday. 7:30 AM. His daughter calls the center line at 6:58 AM. *He fell. Again.* She doesn’t use the radio. Drivers text me personally. The radio logs are reviewed quarterly. Unscheduled stops count as violations. I carry two phones. I delete the texts nightly. The microwave in the break room vents directly into the intake for the main hall HVAC. Reheated salmon triggers the motion-sensor air freshener. *Pine. Pine. Pine.* The scent fights the smell of antiseptic and old skin. I update the whiteboard. *CHEN — 0730 — EXCEPT.* The marker squeaks.

Row 14. The favor. Driver Mike. He needs the court ride for his nephew. Tuesday. 1:00 PM. Juvenile. Shoplifting. *Keep it off the log,* he texts. *I owe you.* I owe him. He covered my shift when my mother fell. He knows I know the spreadsheet better than the director. He knows I know the director skims the transport budget. The whiteboard strip is magnetic. It holds a single paperclip. A receipt from the dollar store. *Laminating sheets. 10 pack. $1.25.* I bought them with petty cash. The director signed the requisition without looking. The strip subtracts something small each time it solves a problem. A sliver of magnetism. A fraction of adhesion. The corners curl.

Row 15. The director. She calls me into her office. The air freshener sprays *Pine* as we enter. *Discrepancies,* she says. *Row 12. Row 13. Row 14.* She taps the printed spreadsheet. *The software shows blanks.* I don’t tell her the software is broken. I don’t tell her I fix it manually. I don’t tell her the drivers trust me because I pretend not to know too much. *Typing error,* I say. *I’ll correct it.* She nods. *Good girl.* The group forgives the person who makes the system legible. The drivers. The nurses. The daughters. The director. They all forgive me.

Row 16. Mrs. Alvarez’s grandson. He waits by the van. He hands me a laminated card. *Medicaid transport authorization. Expires Friday.* The plastic is thick. The corners are rounded. A proof-of-status object. It subtracts something small each time it solves a problem. A day of eligibility. A dollar of reimbursement. A minute of my patience. I clip it to the whiteboard strip with the paperclip. The magnet holds. *ALVAREZ — 0515 — EXCEPT — AUTH.* I text Driver Mike on the center phone. *Alvarez ready. 0515. Auth attached.* I delete the text on my personal phone. *Done.* The microwave beeps in the break room. *Pine.* I sign the receipt for the laminating sheets. The director’s signature is already there. I hand the clipboard back to the grandson. *Have a good day, ma’am.* He doesn’t look at me. He gets in the van. The door folds shut. The engine turns over. The spreadsheet row turns green.
