# The Ledger

Hold the pen vertical. Press hard enough to transfer through three sheets. The top copy goes to the debtor, the middle to the file, the bottom stays bound. This is the only order that matters.

Monday. 08:47. Marta V., 63. Tremor reason: "arthritis flare." Actual reason: her grandson's name on the deed transfer. She signed anyway. The carbon copy shows her signature twice — once shaky, once steady, both in my hand.

The waiting area smells of packing tape and cardboard dust. Boxes stacked against the back wall: ashes, tax returns, a ceramic frog collection. The line stretches past the counter into the retail floor — Amazon returns, birthday gifts, a man shipping his ex-wife's winter coats. They breathe down my neck while I witness.

"Take your time," I say. My voice comes out level. Practiced.

Tuesday. 11:02. Derek K., 29. Tremor reason: "too much coffee." Actual reason: the business loan requires his father's cosignature. Father died in March. Derek forged the name yesterday, practices it now on a receipt. The carbon catches both versions. Mine appears beside them, witnessing a signature I never saw.

The ledger refuses the distinction between witnessed and fabricated. It records what passes through it. Three hundred forty-seven entries since January. Forty-three show this doubling. I stopped counting the ones that don't.

Wednesday. 14:15. Elena R., 41. Tremor reason: "nerves." Actual reason: she's signing her sister out of the family house. The sister sits in the parking lot, engine running. Elena's hand shakes hard enough to tear the top sheet. The carbon beneath stays pristine — her signature, clean and certain, dated tomorrow.

I stamp the document. The notary seal embosses raised letters into paper fibers. Official. Binding. The ledger accepts it.

Thursday. 09:33. The door chimes. I don't look up.

"Marcus."

The pen stops. Ink pools.

"Still keeping that book?"

Marcus leans against the doorframe. Same canvas jacket. Same knuckle tattoo fading into blur. He ran the title office three blocks over until the state consolidated. Now he drives for a logistics firm. Comes in every few months for a notarized affidavit, a power of attorney, the paperwork of other people's crises.

"Still driving?"

"Still driving." He sets a folded paper on the counter. "Need a witness. Not a notary. Just a witness."

The distinction matters. A witness testifies to presence. A notary testifies to identity. The ledger has no column for witness.

"Who's the document for?"

"Me." He pushes it closer. "Bankruptcy discharge. Judge needs someone who knew me before."

Before. The word sits in the air between us. Before the consolidation. Before his wife left. Before I started keeping the refusal book.

The carbonless sheets breathe. The bottom copy darkens — my signature, not yet written, appearing in pressure marks from pages above. Tomorrow's date.

I pick up the pen. Vertical. Press hard.

"Take your time," I say.

Marcus doesn't move. "You never did. For me."

The line behind him shifts. A woman adjusts her box of ashes. The cardboard dust catches light.

I sign. The carbon copies. Two signatures on the bottom sheet — mine, and one I don't remember making, dated yesterday.

"Thank you," Marcus says. He folds the paper, tucks it into his jacket. "You always were the honest one."

The door chimes.

The pen rests in my hand. The ledger lies open. The bottom copy shows three signatures now. Mine. Marcus's. And one in my handwriting, dated tomorrow, witnessing a document I haven't seen yet.

"Next," I say.
