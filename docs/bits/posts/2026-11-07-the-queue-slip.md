# The Queue Slip

You want the truth? Fine. But pull up a crate. The floor's cold and my sciatica doesn't negotiate.

It started with the stamp. Not the rubber one — the brass hand-stamp, heated over the spirit lamp until it smelled like singed hair and authority. I was seventeen, standing on a milk crate to reach the counter at the Department of Civic Weights & Measures, Annex C. My father's scale. His pride. A cast-iron balance with a chipped 500-gram test weight he'd won in a poker game from a railway inspector. Green corrosion fingerprint on the edge. He called it his lucky mole.

The inspector that day was a woman named Marta. Hair pinned up with a pencil, ink-stained thumb, the good eye. You know the good eye. That long, silent stare at the beam's oscillation before she'd even pick up the stamp. Vendors held their breath. I saw a butcher weep once when she passed his scale. He'd fed six kids on that certification.

My father's scale failed. The beam stuck. Rust in the knife-edges. Marta didn't say a word. Just wrote VOID in red ink on the queue slip — triplicate, carbon paper, the bottom copy always illegible — and slid it across the counter. My father's hand shook taking it back. He died three months later. Heart gave out carrying a sack of potatoes up the stairs to Mrs. Kowalski's third-floor walk-up. Said the doctor: exertion. I said: shame.

I took the scale. The crate. The pencil. The queue slips.

Forty-three years. I learned the good eye. I learned the rhythm: tap the beam, watch the settle, count three breaths, stamp. *Thwack.* Certified. *Thwack.* Certified. The queue slips piled up in the wooden bins behind me — PASS, FAIL, HOLD, APPEAL — each one a small judgment on someone's livelihood. A baker's flour scale. A fishmonger's hanging balance. A junk dealer's platform scale rusted orange from battery acid. I stamped them all. Obedience rewarded. Curiosity punished. The one vendor who asked *why* the brass standard weighed 500.003 grams got his certification voided and a week in the requeue line. Never saw him again.

The system didn't care about luck. Or moles. Or fathers. It cared about oscillation periods and knife-edge geometry and the inspector's good eye, which I eventually inherited along with the pencil and the spirit lamp.

Last Tuesday, the scale came in. Not my father's. A digital thing. Plastic. Battery compartment corroded green — same shade as the test weight's mole. The vendor was a boy, maybe nineteen. Hands shaking. Queue slip clenched white-knuckled. He needed certification by noon. Rent money. His mother's medicine. The usual litany.

I tested it. Failed. Drift of 2.3 grams under load. I reached for the red ink.

The boy didn't beg. He *waited*. Hands flat on the counter. Breathing slow. Like he knew something.

"Inspector," he said. "The battery. It's not the scale. It's the battery. I tested it on my mother's kitchen scale. Three times. Same drift. New battery fixes it. I have one in my pocket. Cost me four days' wages. Please."

Curiosity. Punishable. But the queue slip — the bottom copy, the illegible one — had a smudge on the carbon. A fingerprint. Green corrosion.

I looked at the boy's hands. Clean. But his jacket cuff — a smear of green.

My father's test weight. The poker game. The railway inspector.

"Four days' wages," I said.

"Four days' wages," he said.

I didn't stamp VOID. I didn't stamp PASS. I took the battery from his pocket — a corroded cylinder, leaking — and swapped it for the fresh one. The scale settled. Zeroed. Perfect.

*Thwack.* PASS.

The boy's knees buckled. He grabbed the queue slip like it was scripture. "Thank you. Thank you. What do I owe you?"

I looked at the brass stamp cooling on the spirit lamp. The pencil in my hair. The bins full of judgments. The crate I'd stood on since seventeen.

"Nothing," I said. "But you owe the next one. The one after you. You watch their hands. You watch the carbon. You stamp what's true."

He nodded. Ran.

Today, the retirement clerk asked for my queue slip. The final one. EXIT INTERVIEW — TRIPLICATE. I handed it over. She read it. Frowned.

"You wrote 'PASS' in the reason field," she said.

"I did," I said.

"That's not a reason."

"It's the only one that matters."

She stamped it. *Thwack.* Certified.

Outside, the boy was waiting. Not the same boy. A girl this time. Plastic scale. Shaking hands. She held a battery wrapped in a handkerchief.

I still have the pencil. The good eye. The crate.

"Step up," I said. "Let's see what you've got."
