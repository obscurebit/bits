# The Gap Lot

My mother wanted to die in the gap. She said it over the phone, takeout tone: "Zero rent. Zero taxes. No address."

I drove from Reno to the high desert pull-off. The gap was a surveyor's sneeze between two grazing allotments—three acres of cheatgrass omitted from every map. I'd found it when a client's GPS glitched. Now I guided "Secret Vista" tours at two hundred a head. It paid the Subaru's transmission.

Mom waited by the car in a windbreaker, clipboard in hand. Seventy, tough as drywall.

"Survey pins," she said. "1982 BLM caps. Unfiled plat. Dusty at the recorder's office let me photograph the index. He owed me from the water board."

"You testified against his pension."

"He owes me. Also—" trunk pop "—generator, composting toilet, shed kit. Twelve-by-sixteen. Insulated. Two-person build."

No power, water, septic, mail, fire department, deed. Just geometry. When I unfolded the county map on the trunk, the shed sank out of sight. I folded it to bring Mom back. We worked with the paper face down.

"July heat," I said.

"Evaporative cooler. Solar. Battery. Three grand. Cash."

"Medical. Falls. Strokes."

"Telehealth. Starlink. McDermitt clinic's forty minutes. Young doctor. Took my history."

She always won on logistics. Ran tribal housing twenty years before the council fired her for "irregularities" involving her nephew's bid.

"Who builds it?"

"You and me. Tomorrow. U-Haul's at the pull-off."

"You didn't ask."

"Telling. Not asking."

Two days. She directed; I lifted. Water on schedule. Pills at noon and six. Complained about screw gun torque. Not her hip, the wind, the forty-degree nights the battery died.

Day three, BLM truck crested the ridge. Ranger Hayes. He'd ticketed me for campfire in '19.

He eyed the shed, Mom's clipboard. "Address?"

"Doesn't exist. Section 14, T42N, R38E. Unpatented. Unsurveyed. Unclaimed."

"You the guide?"

"Sometimes."

"Commercial permit? Structure permit?"

"No. No."

Radio crackled. He hated paperwork. Two civilians, no violence, no drugs, no fire, a shed on land nobody knew existed.

"Thirty days," he said. "Pack it out. Or I file trespass."

He left.

"We'll move it," Mom said. "Modular. Four bolts per wall. Dusty found three more gaps. Same township. Same error."

She handed me a water bottle. "Stay for dinner. Chili. Canned. From the garden."

Terrible chili. Burnt cumin, tough beans. I ate two bowls.

She died eighteen months later. Third shed. Third gap. Starlink connected. Doctor on video. Me holding the phone, wind tearing the tarp.

Funeral in Winnemucca. Dusty cried. Hayes sent a plant.

I still guide the Secret Vista. Don't mention the sheds. Don't mention the gaps. Sometimes at night I drive fence lines checking for the next surveyor's sneeze. Zero rent. Zero taxes. No address.
