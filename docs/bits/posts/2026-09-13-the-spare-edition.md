# The Tin God of Jinnah Road

I swore an affidavit in 2018 stating the tin was a promotional item from a defunct tire company. I have maintained this position under oath, in mediation, and once during a property dispute with my brother-in-law, who wanted the shop. The tin is a rusted Band-Aid box, the kind that held the round patches and the little tube of glue that smelled like a chemistry set. It sits on the third shelf, behind the brake fluid.

The god does not look like a god. It looks like a smear of grey grease and a bent nail. But it speaks in the voice of my late uncle, Khalid, who taught me to true a wheel by feel. It speaks when the shutter is half-down and the CNG queue stretches to the main road.

The contract is oral, witnessed only by the dust motes. Clause One: The customer must ask for the "Special Patch." Clause Two: They must pay exactly twelve rupees. No rounding up. No "keep the change." Clause Three: The tire must be bald enough to show the canvas. If the tread holds, the tin stays shut.

I have turned away a colonel in a Land Cruiser who offered a thousand. I have served a widow on a Honda 70 who counted out coins, her fingers shaking, three rupees short. I put the three rupees in myself. The tin accepted it. The patch held for two monsoons.

The trouble started with the subscription model. A fleet manager from a logistics app — blue logo, venture capital money — wanted a bulk rate. "Fifty bikes," he said. "Monthly retainer. We'll auto-debit."

I told him the tin doesn't take cards. He laughed, thinking I was a luddite. He left his card on the workbench. "Think about it. Scale."

That night, the tin spoke. *He pays for the patch, not the outcome. The contract is per unit. Per soul.*

"Fifty souls is a lot of patches," I said. "I'd have to order stock."

*The stock is not the issue. The clause is the issue. He wants to buy the mercy in bulk. Mercy cannot be inventoried.*

I threw his card in the bin. The next morning, his riders came individually. Twelve rupees each. Bald tires. The tin worked until the tin ran out of patches.

It happened on a Tuesday. A boy, maybe nineteen, delivery box strapped to his back. Canvas showing. Twelve rupees exact. I opened the tin. Empty. Just the bent nail.

"Come back tomorrow," I said. "Stock arrives Wednesday."

He couldn't. He had a rating to maintain. A timer on his phone. He rode off on the canvas.

He made it three kilometers. The rim shattered on a pothole near the underpass. He walked the bike home. I know because his mother came the next day. She didn't ask for a patch. She gave me a tin of homemade halwa. She said, "He tells me you are a fair man."

I ate the halwa. I did not tell her about the contract. I did not tell her I put the three rupees in for the widow, effectively subsidizing the colonel's refusal. I did not tell her the god wears my uncle's voice because I am the one who forgot to visit the grave for Eid.

The tin is still on the shelf. Empty. I keep it because the lease on the shop has a clause: *Fixtures and fittings remaining after vacation become property of the landlord.* My brother-in-law gets the tin. He gets the nail. He gets the voice.

He does not get the three rupees. Those are written off. A loss carried forward.
