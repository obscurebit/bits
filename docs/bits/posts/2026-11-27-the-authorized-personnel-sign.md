# The Authorized Personnel Sign

The laminated sign wouldn't stick to the asbestos-wing door. The adhesive strips, purchased during the Thompson Administration, had long since surrendered to humidity and whatever chemical the county uses to melt ice on the ramps. I held it against the peeling paint, counting to thirty in my head, the way Marisol taught me. The sign slid down anyway, curling at the corners like a dead leaf.

"Davey," Marisol said from the toll booth. She didn't look up from her crossword. "You're letting the cold in."

The toll booth — break room now, really — smelled of burnt coffee and the space heater fighting the microwave for amperage. Marisol, sixty-three, part-time custodian, full-time institutional memory, wore her county parks fleece over a historical society volunteer t-shirt. The two logos fought each other across her chest. She showed up every Tuesday and Thursday to scrape pigeon guano off the vehicle ramps because nobody had cancelled her keycard. The county forgot the ferry terminal existed in 2014. Marisol didn't.

"Sign's defective," I said, retrieving it. "Thompson Administration adhesive. Known issue."

"Everything's a known issue with you." She filled in a square. "Nine letters for 'deferred maintenance.'"

"Hernandez Special." I peeled the old strips off the sign, fingernails catching the laminate edge. "Or the O'Malley, if it's structural."

"Seven letters."

"O'Malley's nine."

"Then it's not that." She didn't ask why I needed the sign on the asbestos-wing door. She knew. The contractors knew. Everyone knew. The sign was the only thing keeping the demolition bids from starting — the historical society claimed the wing for "archival storage," parks claimed it for "future recreational development," and as long as neither could prove access, the asbestos stayed where it was. Cheaper that way. The county commissioners called it "strategic patience." I called it my job.

The contractors arrived at 9:17. Two of them, reflective vests, clipboards, the particular swagger of men who've been told they're essential. They headed straight for the asbestos-wing.

"Authorized personnel only," I said, stepping from the booth. The sign hung crooked on the door, one corner flapping. I'd used the last of the Gonzalez-era epoxy. It would hold until lunch. "County parks jurisdiction. Historical society access requires Form 407-B."

The younger contractor blinked. The older one — Gutierrez, maybe, or the one who looks like a Gutierrez — smiled the smile of someone who's misunderstood me for years.

"Davey," he said. "We talked to Commissioner Chen. She said the wing's cleared for assessment. Asbestos abatement starts Monday."

My thumb found the loyalty card in my pocket. The coffee shop on Main — nine punches, one free. I'd been carrying it since 2019. The shop burned down in 2021. I still carried it. Nine punches. One free. The precedent was binding.

"Chen doesn't have jurisdiction," I said. My voice came out reasonable. Practiced. "Parks and Rec holds the deed. Historical society holds the variance. You need both signatures. Form 407-B and the environmental rider."

Gutierrez's smile thinned. He knew. He'd always known. The misunderstanding was the only thing keeping the terminal standing.

"Davey," Marisol called from the booth. She held up her phone. "Commissioner Chen on the line. She wants to talk to you."

The contractors watched me. The ramps stretched empty behind them, guano gleaming in the weak sun. Marisol's crossword waited, seven letters for deferred maintenance, nine for the lie that held us together.

I walked to the booth. The space heater clicked. The microwave beeped. The breaker held.

"Tell her I'm not here," I said, taking the phone. "Tell her the sign fell down. Tell her I'm fixing it."

Marisol snorted. "She knows you're there, Davey. She knows the sign's fake. She knows we know."

"Then why's she calling?"

"Because," Marisol said, and for the first time in fifteen years, she looked at me like I was the one who didn't understand. "She needs you to misunderstand her for a little longer. The demolition budget expires Friday. If you hold until Monday, the fiscal year turns over. The money disappears. We get another year."

I stared at the phone. The loyalty card pressed against my thigh through my pocket. Nine punches. One free coffee from a shop that didn't exist.

"Friday," I said.

"Friday," she agreed.

I put the phone to my ear. "Commissioner Chen. This is Davey. About that Form 407-B..."
