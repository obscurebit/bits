# The Queue Log

Item 1: The ticket printer jams at 06:17. Damp roll. The fold-and-tap ritual — crease the leading edge, tap the chassis twice on the left side — clears it. You have performed this ritual forty-three times this month. Management counts it as user error. The log shows zero errors. You initial the margin anyway.

Item 2: The booth heater clicks off at 06:22. The fixture above the window flickers, buzzes, holds. Daylight scrapes across the ambulance bay doors, pale and thin. You can see your breath. The thermal paper curls at the edges. You tape a hand-warmer to the printer's feed roller. It works. Do not log this.

Item 3: Mr. Velez arrives at 06:41. Blue hatchback, dented passenger door, expired inspection sticker he peels a little more each week. He knows the meter on Elm jams when it rains. He knows the attendant at Lot C waves delivery vans through after 19:00. He does not know you know he knows. He hands you a crumpled twenty. "Visitor pass. ICU." His wife. Day fourteen. You print the pass. The paper feeds clean. You do not charge the overnight surcharge. The machine does not flag it. The machine flags shame. It prints a faint watermark — a small gray square in the lower corner — when the operator feels pity. You have learned to ignore it. Mr. Velez does not notice. He never notices. He drives toward the garage.

Item 4: The woman in the gray coat arrives at 07:03. No car. She walked from the bus stop. Her ticket shows four hours, twelve minutes. The rate is eighteen dollars. She has eleven dollars and change in a zippered coin purse. She counts it twice. The queue behind her shifts. A man in a fluorescent vest checks his watch. A teenager with a skateboard sighs. The queue has its own morality: pay fast, move on, do not make a scene. The woman says, "She was supposed to be discharged Tuesday." The machine prints a watermark. Resentment. Sharp corners. You tear the ticket in half. "Fifteen even," you say. "Take the change for coffee." The machine prints a third watermark. Care. Rounded edges, almost a circle. The woman stares at the halves. She takes them. She does not say thank you. She walks toward the hospital entrance. The queue exhales.

Item 5: Your friend Mara texts at 07:19. *Cover my 14:00? Kid threw up.* You type *yes* before reading the rest. *Owe you.* You delete *owe you*. The friendship depends on never comparing. The machine would print a ledger. You do not let it.

Item 6: The supervisor's appeal inbox receives six rejected letters today. *Re: Meter 404 — motorist claims jam, photographic evidence shows functional display. Denied.* *Re: Lot C attendant — informal arrangement violates policy. Denied.* *Re: Overnight surcharge waiver — operator discretion exceeded. Denied.* *Re: Thermal paper budget — request for anti-humidity rolls. Denied.* *Re: Booth heater repair — not capital priority. Denied.* *Re: Staffing — single coverage 06:00–14:00 creates liability. Denied.* You draft them in your head while printing passes. You never send them. The supervisor has not visited the booth in eleven months.

Item 7: The teenager with the skateboard pays at 07:44. Exact change. No watermark. The fluorescent vest man pays at 07:46. Credit card. The machine prints a watermark. Shame. Jagged, dark. He forgot his wallet yesterday. You let him go. Today he pays double. You do not mention yesterday.

Item 8: The printer jams at 08:11. Fold. Tap. Feed. Clean.

Item 9: Mr. Velez returns at 11:58. He pays the overnight surcharge without asking. The machine prints no watermark. You watch his brake lights climb the garage ramp.

Item 10: Shift ends at 14:00. Mara arrives at 14:02. She hands you a coffee. Steam rises. The booth smells of sanitizer, stale coffee, and ozone from the printer. You take the cup. You lock the drawer. The thermal paper roll has seventeen tickets left. You do not reorder. You walk out into the thin daylight. The ambulance bay doors slide open. A stretcher comes through. You keep walking.
