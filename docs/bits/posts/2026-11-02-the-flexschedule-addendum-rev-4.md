# THE FLEX-SCHEDULE ADDENDUM (REV. 4)

Effective immediately, all shift-swap requests must be submitted via the Google Form linked in the #general Slack channel. Paper forms in the break room are deprecated. — Management

You will have initialed Box 3b on the Behavior Assessment Checklist for the brindle pit mix, Kennel 12, because the coffee stain — a dark Rorschach left by Diane, the night dispatcher, circa 2014 — obliterated the words "resource guarding." You will have reasoned, in the quiet of the meet-and-greet room, that a dog who surrendered a bully stick to a toddler in the lobby likely possessed a low threshold. You will have been wrong. The threshold was not low. The threshold was a leather couch, donated by a city councilwoman who once sat in the hallway for forty minutes because she whispered "good boy" during Quiet Hours and Diane heard her.

The couch smelled like every dog who ever sat on it: wet fur, anal glands, the specific yeast of chronic ear infections. You will have loved that couch. You will have eaten your lunch on it, a turkey sandwich on rye, while the lease non-renewal notice sat in your locker, unopened, the pink paper curling at the edges like a dried flower.

The speculative inconvenience arrived on a Tuesday. The Flex-Schedule Addendum (Rev. 4) did not merely move the swap process online; it introduced a "fairness algorithm" that weighted seniority, certification level, and — this was the clause nobody noticed until Marcus from Intake screamed at his monitor — "historical swap equity." A hidden variable. A ghost in the spreadsheet. If you had swapped *out* more than you had swapped *in* over the trailing twelve months, the algorithm penalized your future requests. It treated generosity as debt.

You had swapped out seventeen times. You had swapped in twice. The algorithm saw a deficit of fifteen shifts. You saw the leukemia clinic schedule for your mother, every other Tuesday, 10 a.m. to 2 p.m., the drive from the shelter to the medical park, the parking garage fee you paid with the quarters you saved from the vending machine.

The alliance was fragile. It consisted of you, Diane, and the Google Form. The misunderstanding: Diane believed the algorithm was objective. You knew the algorithm was a story Management told itself about fairness. You kept the misunderstanding alive by not telling Diane that you had hacked the Form. A tiny mark — a single character appended to the `shift_id` parameter in the pre-filled link you bookmarked — flipped your "historical swap equity" from -15 to +15. A building-wide consequence: you got every Tuesday off for the rest of the quarter. The system did not log the change. The system only saw a balanced ledger.

You will have sat on the leather couch, eating your sandwich, watching the brindle pit mix ignore a stuffed Kong. The two-way mirror reflected your face, pale in the fluorescent light. You will not have noticed the dog stiffen until the volunteer — a freshman veterinary technology student named Javier — reached for the toy. The snap was quiet. The sound a bone makes when it breaks clean. Javier's scream was not quiet. It violated Quiet Hours. Diane appeared in the doorway, her silhouette framed by the hallway light. She did not look at the blood. She looked at the whiteboard. "Known Triggers," she read, her voice a whisper. "Resource guarding. Added today."

You will have initialed Box 3b. You will have reasoned wrong. You will have hacked the algorithm to keep your mother's Tuesdays clear. You will have broken the rule about the Form. You will not have broken the rule about the couch. You will take the lease notice from your locker tomorrow. You will sign it. You will find an apartment that allows dogs. The algorithm cannot follow you there.
