# The Weigh Station

Mrs. Gable asked for a ride to the depot. She meant the weigh station. She always meant the weigh station. We drove her there seventeen times last year. The concrete apron held puddles that never dried, black mirrors reflecting the scale house roof leaking rust onto the CAT platform. We knew the sound of her purse snap before she opened it. We knew the weight of the folded cash inside.

You watch the weld puddle freeze. You know the sound — a sharp *tick* as the argon shield breaks, the crater solidifying under the hood. The mobile rig operator, a man named Silas who billed by the bead and smelled of argon and old upholstery foam, had welded the inspector's chair back together three times. He kept the cracked porcelain insulator from a 69kV line on his dash as a paperweight. Its glaze was crazed like a dried lake bed. He said it reminded him of the county.

Mrs. Gable didn't ask for a ride. She asked if the truck ran good. She asked if the tires held air. She asked if we'd seen the new inspector, the one with the clean shirt and the clipboard who didn't know the unwritten rule: you don't ask what the weld is for, only whether it holds. Payment arrived in folded cash or a cooler of venison summer sausage. The inspector wanted forms. Silas wanted the chair to hold.

We rehearsed the confrontation in the cab. *Mrs. Gable, the station is decommissioned. The county sold the scales for scrap. You are ninety-two years old. You do not have a load to weigh.* We rehearsed it until the words lost shape. We never said it. We pulled onto the apron. We killed the engine. We watched her step down, orthopedic shoes finding purchase on the wet concrete, purse snapped shut.

The scale house smelled of mildew and mouse droppings. The inspector sat in the welded chair. He didn't look up. *Name and destination,* he said. His pen hovered over a carbon copy form.

Mrs. Gable set the porcelain insulator on the counter. *Paperweight,* she said. *For your forms.*

The inspector picked it up. Turned it over. *Crazed glaze,* he said. *Structural flaw.*

*Holds fine,* Silas said from the doorway. He hadn't been invited. He smelled like argon and smoke. *Welded the chair you're sitting in. Three times. Holds.*

The inspector set the insulator down. *This station is closed,* he said. *No weighing. No waiting. County ordinance 44-B.*

*Weigh me,* Mrs. Gable said. *I got a load.*

*You have a purse,* the inspector said. *And a ride.*

*Weigh the truck,* she said. *Weigh the rig. Weigh the chair. Weigh the debt.*

We looked at the CAT scale platform. The puddles didn't reflect anything useful. The debt was the truck. The debt was the rig. The debt was the chair welded three times. The debt was the rides we gave, the cash we didn't take, the sausage we ate cold from the cooler. The debt was the inspector's clean shirt. The debt was the form he couldn't file.

*Forty-two thousand pounds,* Silas said. *Empty. Forty-two thousand.*

The inspector wrote it down. *Gross weight,* he said. *Tare weight. Net weight. Sign here.*

Mrs. Gable signed. Her hand didn't shake. *Now,* she said. *Weigh me.*

The inspector looked at the scale. Looked at the chair. Looked at Silas. *The platform doesn't work,* he said. *Hasn't worked in three years. The load cells are shot.*

Silas laughed. A short, ugly sound. *You didn't ask what the weld was for,* he said. *You only asked if it holds.*

*It holds,* the inspector said.

*Then get on,* Mrs. Gable said. *And sign for the weight you're carrying.*
