# The Sunday Cut

You grip the cold-room handle, towel-damp palm on metal. Behind you: sawdust, rendered fat, bakery yeast. 11:14 AM Sunday. The regulars served, walk-ins turned away. Only the roast remains—three-rib, twenty-eight days dry-aged, your crosshatch on the fat cap. $142. You'd have taken $120 from Mrs. Bell, but she's in Boca.

You pull the door.

Hinge sighs. Thirty-four degrees hits your face. But the shelves are wrong. No hanging carcasses. A kitchen table. A calendar on a fridge. A woman in a faded Rams sweatshirt turning from a cutting board, knife raised, carrot halted.

She looks like your wife. Same chin, same split cuticle. But hair shorter—shoulders, not the braid since the wedding. And no limp. The hip replacement she screamed through last November never happened here.

"Took you long enough," she says. "Your mother's ETA twelve minutes. Get the horseradish root?"

You stand straddling thresholds. Cold room behind you, normal and empty. Kitchen ahead, humming different.

"The roast," you say.

She glances back. On the counter: the same roast. Same crosshatch. Same fat cap glowing under the light.

"I got it Thursday. Guy on 9th. $115 with marrow bones." She turns to carrots. "Your mother prefers him. Says fat renders sweeter. She'll say yours is dry. She always says it's dry."

Your mother. Alive. Due in twelve minutes. In your kitchen, dead three years. Your wife limps. The roast in your cold room is the last in the neighborhood—you called Kowalski at 7:00, Costco at 7:15. Supply chain broken at the packer. Nebraska strike, Iowa blockade. You lied to three regulars. Kept the roast for your wife. The hip makes standing at the stove agony. She deserves the fat cap.

"You're tracking sawdust," the other wife says. "Boots off. Call your brother. If he brings Beaujolais again I'm pouring it in the ficus."

You look down. Sawdust on toes. Door hanging patient.

"Where's your husband?"

She stops. "Bathroom. Fighting the duck tie your mother bought. Says ducks are for hunters. I say ducks are for people who don't want the Packers for three hours."

Your tie. Hanging unworn since the funeral. Forgotten.

"Tell him the roast is cut. Three ribs. Twenty-eight days. Crosshatch. Ready."

She frowns. "You didn't cut it. Whole. You always roast whole. Bone conducts heat, you say—"

"I know what I say. Tell him anyway."

She studies you. No limp. No scar. No oxycodone bottle. No nights calculating refills, hating the body for breaking.

"Okay. Coffee? You look like hell."

You look at the roast. The only roast. In your cold room it waits, butcher paper, *HOLD - SUNDAY* in your Sharpie.

Your wife texted 9:42: *hip bad. staying in bed. bring it whole. i'll sear slow.*

You replied: *on my way.*

You haven't moved.

"Black."

You step fully in. Door clicks shut behind you. Unlace boots. Sawdust on foreign linoleum. Mother arriving. Brother with wrong wine. Husband with duck tie in bathroom smelling of your sandalwood cologne—stopped wearing it when she said it reminded her of her grandfather's funeral.

Pull chair. Sit at table worn smooth where your elbows rest every night.

"Thanks."

She pours. Steam rises. Calendar shows next week empty. No appointments. No therapy. No pharmacy runs. Just blue pen: *Dad's 70th - reserve Kowalski.*

You wrap hands around the mug. Ceramic burns. You do not let go.
