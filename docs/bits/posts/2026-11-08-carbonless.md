# Carbonless

06:00 — Admission Discharge Transfer Form — ID: Wristband Scan — Fee: $15 — Thumbprint: Y

The helium tank hissed in the gift shop's back hallway, a sound like a tire losing air in slow motion. Mylar balloons sagged at half-mast: *GET WELL*, *THINKING OF YOU*, one *HAPPY BIRTHDAY* that had clearly been repurposed. The envelopes for the cards were gone, stolen or used, leaving the cards flapping open like dead birds.

Mara clipped her spare pen to her badge reel. The patients' hands always shook too much for caps. She'd learned that in month two. She'd also learned to keep the pink copies. The NCR book carbonless duplicate receipt book — white top for the family, yellow middle for the state, pink bottom for her — the pink ones piled up in her glove compartment like receipts from a life she didn't live.

06:17 — Durable Power of Attorney — ID: Witness Credible (Daughter) — Fee: $15 — Thumbprint: Y

The daughter stood in the hallway, arms crossed, smelling of rain gear and toner. Her brother sat in a vinyl chair, scrolling. The patient — their mother — slept three feet away behind a curtain that didn't quite close.

"I'm the credible witness," the daughter said. "I drove her to the clinic. I paid for the parking. I held the cup when she threw up."

"The law says disinterested," Mara said. "You inherit. He inherits. Neither of you is disinterested."

"We'll split it," the brother said without looking up. "Just stamp it."

Mara opened her book. The carbon paper was obsolete technology, the kind the hospital kept ordering because the IT system went down every third Tuesday and the lawyers still trusted ink. She wrote the date. The document type. The fee.

"Fifteen dollars," she said. "Cash or Venmo."

The daughter paid. Venmo. Mara tore the pink copy, pocketed it. The white copy went to the daughter. The yellow stayed in the book.

06:42 — Advance Directive — ID: Witness Credible (Neighbor) — Fee: $15 — Thumbprint: Y

The neighbor was a woman named Mrs. Kato who brought tupperware of onigiri to the nurses' station every Tuesday. She didn't want the fee. She wanted Mara to know the patient — Mr. V, room 314 — had once told her he'd rather die than live in a home. He'd said it over the fence, trimming hedges. He'd said it three years ago.

"He changed it last week," Mrs. Kato said. "The directive. His son made him. I heard them arguing."

Mara looked at the form. The son's signature was already on it, witnessed by a traveling notary from a competing service. The date was four days old.

"Did he have capacity?" Mara asked.

"His hands shook," Mrs. Kato said. "But his voice didn't."

Mara's pen hovered. The carbonless paper waited. She could refuse. She could call the state board. She could do the thing that felt like justice and watch Mrs. Kato lose the only person who brought her onigiri, watch the son inherit the house and sell it, watch the directive stand because a form signed four days ago beat a conversation from three years ago every time.

The fairest option felt cruel because it arrived too late.

07:05 — HIPAA Authorization — ID: Witness Credible (Son) — Fee: $15 — Thumbprint: Y

The son from room 314 stood in the hallway. He smelled like someone else's lunch — curry, maybe, reheated badly. He held a phone with a cracked screen.

"I need the records," he said. "The insurance company. They're denying the claim. They say the fall wasn't covered."

Mara nodded. She wrote. She didn't ask about the fall. She didn't ask about the directive. She didn't ask why the neighbor's onigiri tasted better than his cooking.

"Fifteen dollars," she said.

He paid cash. Exact change. Coins warm from his pocket.

Mara tore the pink copy. Pocketed it. Handed him the white.

"Thank you," he said.

She clipped the pen back to her badge reel. The helium tank hissed. A *GET WELL* balloon drifted sideways, bumping the wall.

07:12 — Journal Reconciliation — Entries: 4 — Fees Collected: $60 — Pink Copies Retained: 4 — Thumbprints: 4

She sat in her car in the parking structure, engine off, the pink copies fanned across the steering wheel. Four signatures. Four fees. Four moments where someone needed an apology but could only safely ask for a receipt.

She started the engine. Drove to the taco truck on 7th. Ordered two carne asada, one al pastor. Paid with a twenty. The girl at the window gave her change and a carbonless receipt — white, yellow, pink.

Mara tore off the pink copy. Handed it back through the window.

"You keep it," she said.

The girl blinked. "It's yours."

"Consider it a favor," Mara said. "I'm collecting them."

The girl laughed, took it back, tucked it into her apron pocket. "Weirdo."

Mara drove off, eating a taco with one hand, the other on the wheel. The receipt book sat in the passenger seat, pink copies growing like moss. She'd need a new book by Friday. She'd order it tonight. The state required sequential numbering. She never skipped a number.
