# The Token Log

November 23, 1946. Boiler Room.

The brass token sits warm in my palm, smaller than a quarter, stamped GOOD FOR ONE WASH — NO CASH VALUE on one side, worn smooth on the other. Mrs. Chen from 3B pressed it into my hand twenty minutes ago, her fingers damp with detergent. "For the nurse in 1A," she whispered. "Her shift ends at midnight. She has no quarters."

I pocketed it. Logged it. Line 47: Token received — Chen, 3B. Destination: 1A. Purpose: shift coverage.

The new efficiency contractor, Mr. Harlow, wants the boilers ripped out by spring. "Modern oil," he says. "No more coal dust. No more you." He stood in this doorway last Tuesday with his clipboard and his clean fingernails and told me the laundry machines are next. "Quarter slots are obsolete. Token systems invite theft." He doesn't know the tokens don't work in any machine but ours. He doesn't know Mrs. Chen has been leaving two tokens every Tuesday for eighteen months, and the nurse in 1A leaves a folded egg salad sandwich on the folding table every Wednesday, and neither woman has ever spoken the other's name.

I know because I watch. I know because I'm the one who unjams the machines when 1980s quarters gum the works — someone's kid found a roll in a grandfather's drawer, now the whole building pays for it. I know because I'm the one who wakes at 3 AM when the radiator in 2C bangs like a trapped animal, and the child in that room hasn't grown an inch in three years. I measured. Doorframe pencil marks. Same height. November 1943. November 1944. November 1945. November 1946.

The nurse in 1A — her shift ends at midnight. But the token Mrs. Chen gave me? The stamp reads GOOD FOR ONE WASH. The nurse's shift ended at 11:47 PM. I know because I heard her key in the lock. I know because the sandwich appeared on the folding table at 11:48. Warm bread. Fresh mayo. The token system records what everyone performed. Not what happened.

Line 48: Token redeemed — 1A. Time: 11:47 PM. Discrepancy: 13 minutes.

Mr. Harlow's clipboard has no column for discrepancies. His efficiency report has no row for the night-shift nurse who clocks out before her shift ends, or the child who doesn't grow, or the superintendent who knows which radiators bang at 3 AM and which tenants pay rent in crumpled bills stuffed behind loose baseboards.

I slide the token into the coffee can under the workbench. Forty-seven others already there. Brass disks catching basement light.

The boiler groans. Steam hisses through a patched seam I welded with scrap copper and prayer. Three dollars and twelve cents in patches this year. I logged that too. Line 12: Boiler repair — copper sleeve, flux, labor. Cost: $3.12. Reimbursement: pending. Mr. Harlow says the new boiler pays for itself in eighteen months. He doesn't ask who pays the twelve cents.

Mrs. Chen's token burns in the can. The nurse's sandwich sits uneaten on the warped folding table. The child in 2C will wake at 3 AM when the radiator bangs, same height as last November, same height as the November before.

I pick up my pen. Line 49: Radiator 2C — bang cycle 0300. Tenant: child. Growth: 0 inches. Duration: 36 months.

The log doesn't fix anything. But it remembers what the efficiency report erases.

Mr. Harlow's new boiler arrives in March. I'll be gone by April. The tokens will stop circulating. The sandwiches will stop appearing. The nurse will clock out at midnight like a normal person, and the child in 2C will finally grow, and Mrs. Chen will keep her tokens in a jar like buttons.

I close the logbook. My knuckles ache. The boiler groans again, deeper this time. Something giving way.

Thirteen minutes. That's what the token stole. Thirteen minutes the nurse didn't work but got paid for. Thirteen minutes Mrs. Chen bought with brass and silence. Thirteen minutes I logged while the system redesigns me out.

The cost isn't the boiler. Isn't the tokens. Isn't even the child who doesn't grow.

The cost is that I'll miss the 3 AM bang. That I'll lie in whatever room they move me to, listening to a silent radiator, waiting for a sound that never comes.
