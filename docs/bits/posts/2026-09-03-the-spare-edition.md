# The Lens Audit

My mother left her frames on the nightstand the night she died. Cheap wire rims, bent at the bridge from falling asleep reading. My father wore them for three years. Said the prescription was close enough. Said she liked him in them.

The optician, a woman named Pilar with a mole on her chin and a laminated flowchart taped to her wall, slid the glasses across the counter. "Standard revision lease," she said. "Twelve-month term. You'll need the son's signature for the bilateral clause."

"I'm not signing," I said.

My father didn't look at me. He polished the lenses with his shirttail. "It's one argument, Mark. The one about the boat. You remember it wrong."

"I remember it," I said. "I was there. You were there. Mom was there. Three witnesses."

"Two," he said. "She doesn't count anymore."

Pilar tapped her pen on the flowchart. "Gentlemen. The bilateral clause requires both parties to experience the revised memory from the opposing perspective. Duration: four minutes, seventeen seconds. Cost is split fifty-fifty unless one party earns under twenty-five thousand, then it's prorated. Your father qualifies. You don't."

"I make twenty-six thousand," I said.

"Twenty-six two," she corrected. "Tax return from last April. I have it on screen."

My father put the glasses on. The frames pinched his temples. "Sign the paper, Mark. I need to remember why I said those things. From your side."

"You said them because you're a bastard."

"Four minutes," Pilar said. "You'll feel a cold pressure behind the left eye. That's the perspective anchor. Don't fight it."

I signed. My father signed. Pilar swapped the left lens for a clear blank etched with a serial number. The right lens stayed my mother's prescription.

"Sit," she said.

We sat in the two vinyl chairs by the window. The street outside was wet. A bus hissed past.

The cold pressure arrived. Not painful. Just a sudden knowing: I was my father, fifty-eight, standing in the garage holding a varnish brush. The boat hull smelled of epoxy and old pine. My son — me — stood in the doorway, arms crossed, saying the varnish would trap rot. Saying I didn't know what I was doing. Saying I never finished anything.

The shame was physical. Hot in the face. Tight in the throat. I — he — wanted to throw the brush. Wanted to say something cruel about the son's failed marriage, the bankruptcy, the way he visited twice a year and complained about the drive.

But he didn't. He set the brush down. He said, "You're right. Show me."

The pressure released. The vinyl chair scratched my back. My father sat beside me, breathing hard, his hands white on the armrests.

"Well?" he asked.

I looked at the glasses in his lap. The left lens clear. The right lens smudged with his thumbprint.

"You didn't say it," I said. "The cruel thing. You didn't say it."

He stared at the floor. "No. I didn't."

Pilar collected the paperwork. "Lease activated. First payment due in thirty days. Late fees apply after five."

We walked to the parking lot. My father's truck was a nineteen ninety-four Ford, rust eating the wheel wells. He fished his keys out with trembling fingers.

"Drive safe," I said.

He paused. The dome light clicked on as he opened the door. "You came to the launch," he said. "Three years later. You brought beer. You didn't say a word about the varnish."

"I remember," I said.

He got in. The engine turned over on the third try. As he pulled away, I saw him reach for the glasses on the dash. Put them on. Adjust the bridge. The right lens caught the streetlight. The left stayed clear.
