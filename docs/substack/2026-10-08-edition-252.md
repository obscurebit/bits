### The Queue at Counter Six

You're late. The number machine spat a ticket at 4:58 p.m., pink cardstock curling at the edges like a burnt photograph, and the waiting room already holds the shape of your evening: plastic chairs bolted to linoleum, a water cooler gurgling empty, the clerk behind the glass partition moving with the deliberate slowness of someone who has survived thirty years of "I just have a quick question."

She doesn't look up. Her nameplate reads M. VARGAS, LIEN CLERK II. The roman numerals are scratched out with ballpoint, rewritten in block letters: III. Someone cared enough to correct it. No one cared enough to order a new plate.

"Number forty-seven," she says. Her voice doesn't rise. It doesn't need to. The room hears anyway.

You're forty-nine. The man holding forty-six — gray polo, knees dusted with drywall powder — shifts his weight. His ticket is torn at the corner. He's been here before. They all have. This is the County Annex basement, where sidewalk assessments come to die. The fluorescent fixture above the rolling ladder flickers once, twice, settles into a hum that vibrates in your molars. The ladder hasn't moved from the '98-'04 shelf bay in six years. You know because you've watched. You've had time.

The printer behind her — TallyGenicom 6300, yellowed as old teeth — chugs awake. Continuous-feed paper unfurls, perforations tearing ragged where the tractor-feed sprockets miss every twelfth tooth. *Chuck-chuck-chuck-hiss.* The sound of money owed. The sound of your sidewalk, your neighbor's sidewalk, the crack you tripped on last November that the city measured, photographed, assessed, and forgot to bill until the lien attached itself to your property tax like a barnacle.

"Forty-seven," Vargas says again.

Drywall knees approaches. He slides a folder through the slot. She doesn't touch it immediately. Her fingers — nails bitten to the quick — hover over the FoxPro database on the monitor beside her. The auto-import choked on handwritten parcel IDs again. She spends every lunch break typing them in manually, parcel by parcel, assessment by assessment, because the cloud parcel viewer won't accept ink on paper. You know this because you asked once. She told you while the printer *chuck-chuck-chucked* behind her, not looking up, not stopping.

"It's not my job," she'd said. "But it's my queue."

The union steward — a man named Dale who smells of menthol cough drops and keeps a laminated grievance form in his breast pocket — insisted the printer stay "mission critical" to protect her half-step pay grade. IT schedules its removal every Tuesday. She cancels the work order every Monday. You've seen the emails printed and stacked beside her keyboard. Subject: *Legacy Hardware Decommission*. Body: *Per directive 94-12.*

She types. Parcel 144-022-019. Assessment: $2,340. Interest accruing since 2003. The screen refreshes. The cloud accepts it. The printer *chuck-chuck-chucks* a receipt.

"Paid in full," she says. Drywall knees exhales. His shoulders drop three inches. He takes the receipt like it's a pardon.

"Forty-eight."

A woman in a cardigan the color of bruised plums steps up. She doesn't have a folder. She has a shoebox. Taped shut. She sets it on the counter with reverence.

"Everything since '99," she says. "My husband handled it. He died in March."

Vargas nods. Once. She takes the box. Opens it. Receipts, canceled checks, a letter from the city dated 2001 promising the assessment would be "re-evaluated." The re-evaluation never came. The lien grew. The interest compounded. The cloud parcel viewer shows a balance of $18,700.

The printer *chuck-chuck-chucks.*

Vargas types. Parcel by parcel. Check by check. The shoebox empties. The screen refreshes. The cloud accepts. The printer spits a final receipt: *BALANCE: $0.00.*

The woman in the cardigan presses her fingers to her mouth. She doesn't cry. She breathes.

"Forty-nine."

You step up. Your ticket curls worse than the others. You slide your folder through the slot. The assessment for your sidewalk: $4,200. You have the money. You brought a cashier's check. You want this done. You want to walk out of here without owing anyone anything.

Vargas opens your folder. She doesn't type. She stares at the parcel ID. Handwritten. Faded. The auto-import would choke.

"You know," she says, still not looking at you, "the sprockets miss every twelfth tooth. Has since '97. Dale says it's mission critical. IT says it's a hazard. I say it's the only thing in this building that counts honestly."

She types. *Chuck-chuck-chuck-hiss.* The receipt slides through the slot.

You take it. *BALANCE: $0.00.*

"Next," she says. "Fifty."

You walk out. The fluorescent fixture flickers. The ladder doesn't move. The printer *chuck-chuck-chucks* for number fifty, and fifty-one, and fifty-two, counting honestly, one ragged perforation at a time.
