### The Long Course

The epoxy smell bit the back of the throat, sharp and chemical-sweet. Marta rolled the long-handled roller across the pool bottom, the blue paint sealing over the old hash marks. Her father, Silas, walked the gutter line with a shop-vac hose, sucking up the grit the pressure washer missed. He moved slow, favoring the left knee. Sixty-two years of concrete and chlorine had worn the cartilage thin.

"Lane four," Silas said, voice flat over the vacuum whine. "Short course yards. Twenty-five even. You measure it?"

Marta checked the laser level on the tripod. "Twenty-five point zero. Dead on."

She rolled the stripe. White, crisp, two inches wide. The pool was empty, the air heavy and still. The city had drained it for the annual refit. She had taken the week off from the dispatch desk — unpaid, no benefits — to help him finish the municipal contract. His last contract. The Parks Department had already awarded the next three years to a drone-spray collective out of Zagreb. Cheaper. No pensions to fund.

"Good," Silas said. He clicked off the vac. "Pack it. We're done."

They loaded the rollers, the tape, the tripod into the bed of his truck. The cab smelled like stale coffee and liniment. He drove the service road out to the highway, the suspension groaning over the potholes. He didn't turn toward her apartment. He turned toward the airport industrial park.

"Dad. The house is west."

"New site," he said. "County aquatics center. Annex B. They need the long course meters verified before the certifiers show up Tuesday."

"Your cert expired," she said. "Last month. You told me."

"Reciprocity agreement," he said. "Provisional. If I walk the tank tonight, sign the affidavit, the ticket stays live for sixty days. Long enough to vest the pension bump."

"You're retired," she said. "You signed the papers. I saw the PDF."

"I signed the *separation* papers," he corrected. "Not the *vesting* waiver. There's a delta."

She watched the warehouses blur past. The truck's nav screen flickered with a route she didn't recognize: a private access road behind a logistics fence, marked `MAINT_Access_7/UNVERIFIED`. "Does the union know?"

"The union rep is the one who sent the ping," Silas said. "Encrypted channel. Said the drone collective's survey data drifted. I went yesterday. Watched the far wall move four centimeters while my tape was hooked to it. Same concrete, same gutter. Then the address on the entrance changed. This morning my pension letter had your survivor benefit missing."

"And if you sign?"

"Pension vests at the Tier Three rate. Twelve hundred more a month. Medical stays active. You get the survivor benefit."

The truck bounced down a rutted gravel drive. Ahead, a massive tilt-up concrete box sat dark behind a chain-link fence. A single sodium vapor light buzzed over a roll-up door. No signage. Just a QR code stenciled on the steel, weathered to gray.

Silas killed the engine. "I need a witness. Independent. Non-union. Blood relative qualifies under the '22 amendment. You hold the tape. I walk the line. We both sign. Takes forty minutes."

Marta looked at the fence. The QR code. The dark building. She thought of the dispatch queue blinking red on her phone, the gig-app shifts she'd need to scrape rent, the certification course for crane signaling she couldn't afford. She thought of the epoxy smell in her nails.

"Four centimeters," she said. "Per lane. That's nothing."

"In the water, it's everything," Silas said. He opened the door. "Coming?"

She unbuckled. The night air was cold, smelling of diesel and wet dirt. "Yeah. I'm coming."
