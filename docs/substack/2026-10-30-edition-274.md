## 📖 Today's Bit

### The Ledger in the Back Room

"Marlowe, I need you to lie to a dead man's sister."

Eddie didn't look up from the thermal printer. The thing squeaked like a dying mouse every time it spat a label. "I don't lie to family. I lie to PayPal. There's a difference."

"She thinks the store credit is cash. Twelve hundred dollars sitting in a defunct Mercari account. If I tell her it's non-transferable store credit, she'll hire a lawyer. If you tell her it's 'processing,' she waits." He tapped the laminated Quick Reference card clipped to the monitor. Coffee stain on the eBay API deprecation row. "You owe me for the notary stamp last month. The one that jammed on the Henderson affidavit."

The notary stamp thunked on the counter. Rhythm section for the printer's squeak. This back room at the UPS Store on 4th and Main smelled like packing tape and old cardboard. Quiet enough that every breath counted.

"Fine," I said. "But I'm not calling her 'ma'am.'"

***

The probate clerk at Superior Court, Room 312, didn't blink. She'd seen my wet-ink notarized affidavits before. Knew the routine: print, sign, scan, upload, watch the platform reject for 'illegible signature,' repeat.

"Mercari account #M-44721," she read. "Date of death: March 3. Affidavit notarized..." She squinted at the stamp. "Yesterday. By Edward R. Voss."

"Same notary," I said. "Same back room."

She stamped RECEIVED. "Twelve to fourteen weeks for the check. Made payable to the estate."

"Estate doesn't exist. No will. Sister's the only heir. She needs cash for the funeral."

"Then she waits." The clerk slid the file into her drawer. "Next."

***

Eddie's desk, two hours later. He was peeling a shipping label off his thumb.

"She called," he said. "The sister. Wanted to know why 'processing' takes twelve weeks."

"What'd you tell her?"

"Truth. Platform policy. Inactivity Policy v.4.2, effective January '39. Accounts dormant eighteen months convert to non-transferable credit. Death doesn't restart the clock." He shrugged. "She cried. Hung up. Called back twenty minutes later asking for you by name."

"She doesn't know my name."

"She described you. 'The quiet one who actually answers.'"

The printer squeaked. A fresh label curled into the tray.

***

Her name was Doris. She sat across from me in the back room, purse clutched like a life raft. Gray wool coat. Frayed cuffs. She smelled like lavender and cigarettes.

"Mr. Marlowe. Edward said you'd explain."

"Edward's optimistic."

"He said the money's real. Just... stuck."

"Twelve hundred in store credit. Mercari credit. Can't withdraw. Can't transfer. Can only spend on the platform." I slid the laminated card toward her. "Policy v.4.2. Your brother stopped selling in '37. Account went dormant. Credit locked."

She stared at the coffee stain. "He was saving for something. Wouldn't say what. Just kept listing things. Old tools. His father's watch. A set of carving chisels." Her fingers traced the binder clip. "He died before he bought it."

"Bought what?"

"Didn't say. But the chores... he'd talk about 'when the account clears.' Like it was a bank."

"It's not a bank."

"I know that now." She folded her hands. "Can you buy the chisels? Ship them to me? I'll pay you cash."

"Mercari doesn't ship tools over fifty pounds. And the seller's gone. Account's frozen."

"Then what good is it?"

The notary stamp thunked. Eddie, stamping something behind me.

"Nothing," I said. "That's what I came to tell you."

She nodded. Slow. Like she'd known. "Edward said you were honest. Said that's why he sent me to you. Said you'd help me understand."

"I'm not helping. I'm closing the file."

"But you understand." She stood. Coat sleeve caught the card, flipped it. The back had my handwriting: *v.4.3 drops June. API kills legacy credit conversion. If you're reading this, you're already late.*

She read it. Read it again.

"June," she whispered. "That's... that's next month."

"July, the credit vanishes. Platform sunset."

She looked at me. Really looked. "You knew. When Edward asked you to lie. You knew it was disappearing."

"I knew the policy."

"And you let me think there was time."

The printer squeaked. A long, dying sound.

"I let you think there was a solution," I said. "There isn't one."

She left the purse. Left the coat. Walked out into the 4th Street noise without them.

***

Eddie didn't ask. Just handed me a fresh affidavit. Henderson estate. Stripe account. API deprecation v.2.1.

"She'll come back," he said. "For the stamp. For the notary. She'll need someone to witness."

"She won't."

"Everyone comes back. That's the job."

I picked up the pen. Wet ink. The only thing the platforms still trusted.

"Yeah," I said. "But not for this."

The stamp thunked. The printer squeaked. In the quiet back room, I signed a name that wasn't mine to a document that wouldn't work for a woman who'd already lost everything, and I finally understood what Eddie had paid me for.

Not the lie.

The witness.
