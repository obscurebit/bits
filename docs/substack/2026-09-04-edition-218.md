## 📖 Today's Bit

### The Drain Schedule

So I'm down in the hypocaust of the Baths of Caracalla, right? July, ninety degrees, lying on my back in two inches of sludge, chipping Roman hydraulic cement off a terra-cotta pipe with a hammer and chisel because the Parks Department won't spring for a rotary tool. My knees are wrecked. My back is wrecked. The foreman, Giulio, he's up top eating a panino and screaming about the irrigation timer for the cypress trees.

"Antonio! The zone-four valve! It sticks! Fix it or the trees die!"

The trees. Always the trees. Two thousand years of empire, aqueducts, concrete that heals itself, and the city kills a cypress hedge because a forty-euro solenoid sticks.

I wipe my eyes, crawl out the access hatch, blinking into the sun. The tourists are thick, selfie-sticks clicking. I walk past the ticket booth, past the gift shop selling sponge replicas, to the utility shed by the north wall. The controller box is rusted, the lid held on by a bungee cord. I pop it open. Zone four. I twist the solenoid. Click. Water hammers in the pipes. Good. Done.

I'm wiping my hands on my jeans when I see it.

Down in the valve box, below the pipe fittings, where the gravel meets the clay.

A city.

Not a model. A city. Three inches square, maybe four. Towers of something like bone, or fired clay, sharp as needles. Streets laid out in a grid that hurts to look at, perfectly straight, draining into a central sump that is exactly the main drain pipe I just cleaned. Tiny aqueducts, hair-thin, bringing water from the condensation on the valve box lid. Little fields, terraced on the gravel slope, growing something purple and mossy. And figures. Moving. Millimetric. Carrying things. Arguing, maybe. Gesturing at the sudden flood.

I stare. My heart does a weird stutter.

Then Giulio's voice cracks over the radio on my belt. "Antonio! The baths! The fountain in the *frigidarium*! It's running backwards! Tourists are complaining!"

I look at the city. The flood is receding. They're already digging ditches. Re-routing. Fast. Efficient. They don't panic. They *manage*.

I look at the radio. Then at the valve box. Then at the purple crop, half-drowned.

"Antonio? You hear me?"

"Yeah, Giulio. Heard you."

I reach into my tool bag. I pull out the roll of blue polyethylene tubing we use for drip lines. I pull out the box of tiny barbed emitters. The cheap plastic ones. Ten cents each.

I sit on the hot gravel. My knees scream. I spend the next hour running micro-lines from the zone-four discharge, tapping them into the gravel *beside* their fields, not on them. Adjusting the flow with a thumb screw until it's a gentle mist. I build them a tiny settling tank from a plastic yogurt cup I find in the trash. I connect their main drain to the overflow with a piece of surgical tubing from the first-aid kit.

They watch. A few come to the edge of their world, where the gravel meets the plastic. They hold up... tools? Hands? Hard to say. They make a sound. Like static. Like grit in a bearing.

"Grazie," I whisper. "Don't mention it."

I close the valve box lid. Snap the bungee cord.

"Fixed it, Giulio," I say into the radio. "Zone four's good. Fountain's good. Just a blockage."

"Finally. Get back up here. The Director wants a selfie with the mosaic."

I stand up. My joints pop. I pick up my hammer.

"Sure thing, boss," I say. "Coming up."

But I don't go up the stairs. I walk to the far corner of the site, where the oleanders grow wild. I piss in the dirt. And while I do, I swear I hear, rising from the valve box below my boots, a sound like a thousand tiny hammers on tiny anvils. Building. Already building.
