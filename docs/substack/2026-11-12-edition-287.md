## 📖 Today's Bit

### The Warrant

0600  -4°C  CALM  CLR

The ink feathered on the log sheet. Mara pressed harder, the ballpoint tearing the fiber. Her knuckles were raw from the gravel crouch — knees down, forehead to the louvered slats, reading the max/min thermometers inside the Stevenson screen. The alcohol column stuck at minus four. She tapped the glass. Nothing. She tapped again. The column jumped to minus three. She wrote minus three.

0700  -3°C  CALM  CLR

The brass sling psychrometer lived in a leather case that smelled of saddle soap and mineral deposits. The muslin wicks were stiff as cardboard. She wet them from the thermos, swung the instrument sixty seconds, read the wet-bulb depression, calculated relative humidity on the slide rule. Forty-two percent. She logged it. The slide rule's cursor caught on a burr of dried varnish. She spat on her thumb, rubbed the track smooth.

0800  -2°C  LIGHT NW  FEW

Deeter's truck coughed up the access road, diesel cloud hanging in the inversion layer. He killed the engine. The silence that followed was heavier than the noise.

"County warrant's at the feed store," he said, climbing out. His coveralls were stained at the cuffs with something red-brown — transmission fluid, maybe, or the clay from the lower pasture. "Mrs. Varga's holding it. Said she'd deduct the twine before she'd hand over cash."

Mara didn't look up from the anemometer cups. Frozen stiff. She kicked the post. One cup turned, grudging. "Twine and the fence posts."

"Just the twine. Posts were last month." Deeter leaned against the instrument shelter, rolling a cigarette. Tobacco shredded onto his lip. "You going to sign?"

"Have to. Mortgage doesn't care about principle."

"Principle." He lit up, cupping the flame. "That's the word for it now? Last month it was 'the arrangement.' Month before that 'the understanding.'"

0900  -1°C  LIGHT NW  SCT

The arrangement. The understanding. The lie.

Three years ago, the NWS announced automation. ASOS units. Satellite uplink. The cooperative observer program would sunset. Mara's stipend — forty dollars a month, paid quarterly via county warrant — would vanish. Deeter's too. They were the last two observers in the district.

So they'd agreed: keep reporting. Hand-written logs. Mailed forms. The telegraph key in the basement had gone silent in '87, but the forms still asked for transmission time. Mara wrote 0600Z every day. Deeter wrote 0600Z every day. Neither had sent a telegram since Reagan's first term.

The NWS field office in Denver didn't know. The forms arrived, the data entered some database nobody checked. The warrants kept coming.

1000  0°C  MOD NW  BKN

Deeter's wife called at noon. Mara heard the receiver clunk against the kitchen wall, the muffled shout: "Tell her I'm alive! Tell her the checks clear!"

Deeter's mother. Eighty-four. In a home in Greeley. She believed her son worked for the Weather Service. A real job. Federal benefits. A pension coming. Deeter sent her fifty dollars a month from the warrant money and called it a pension disbursement. He'd printed a fake stub on the library's dot-matrix printer. Laminated it with contact paper.

"She asks about the satellite thing," Deeter said later, wiping grease from his hands with a rag. "Says she read in the paper the stations are closing. Says maybe I should retire."

"She thinks you have a pension."

"She thinks I *earned* a pension. Difference."

1100  +1°C  MOD NW  OVC

The psychrometer's wicks were bone dry again. Mara wet them, swung, calculated. Thirty-eight percent. The air was drying out. Snow would sublimate before it melted. The pasture would go into spring with nothing.

She thought of the feed store. Mrs. Varga's scale. The way the warrant's numbers blurred under the endorsement stamp. Mara's tab: fence posts, baling twine, a new handle for the snow shovel, the veterinary bill when the heifer calved breech. The warrant covered it all, with twelve dollars left over. Twelve dollars bought coffee and cat food for two weeks.

1200  +2°C  MOD NW  OVC

Deeter packed his thermometer case. Brass sling psychrometer, leather case, slide rule, spare wicks. He set them on the kitchen table beside his laminated pension stub.

"I'm not signing next quarter," he said.

Mara stirred her coffee. The spoon clinked. "The mortgage."

"Let it go. The place is worth more than the note. Bank'll sell it to a developer from Denver. They'll put in a subdivision. Call it 'Weatherstone Estates.'"

"Deeter."

"I'm serious. I'll drive the truck for the co-op full time. Extension agent said they need a driver for the soil probe route. Three counties. Diesel and seed corn smell. She'll teach me the frost-date chart."

1300  +2°C  FRESH NW  OVC

The extension agent. Mara had met her once. A woman in a truck that smelled of diesel and seed corn, carrying soil probes and a laminated frost-date chart she updated in pencil each spring. She'd looked at Mara's hands — raw knuckles, ink-stained fingers — and said nothing.

1400  +1°C  FRESH NW  BKN

Deeter left at three. His truck coughed down the access road, the diesel cloud lingering.

Mara stayed. She read the thermometers at 1500, 1600, 1700. The temperature fell. The wind shifted north. The sky cleared to scattered.

1800  -1°C  LIGHT N  SCT

She drove to the feed store at dusk. The warrant lay on Mrs. Varga's counter beside the scale. Mrs. Varga didn't look up from her ledger.

"Twine?"

"Twine."

"Fence posts?"

"Not this month."

Mrs. Varga stamped the warrant. *PAID.* She peeled off the twine cost, counted cash into Mara's palm. Twelve dollars.

1900  -2°C  CALM  CLR

Mara sat in her truck in the feed store lot. The warrant stub in her pocket. Twelve dollars. Coffee. Cat food. Two weeks.

She thought of Deeter's mother in Greeley, waiting for a pension that didn't exist. Thought of Deeter driving three counties in a diesel truck, learning frost dates in pencil. Thought of the forms she'd mail Monday, the 0600Z she'd write, the data nobody checked.

She started the engine. The heater blew cold, then warm.

2000  -3°C  CALM  CLR

At home, she opened the logbook to tomorrow's page. She wrote the date. She wrote 0600. She wrote the temperature she knew would be there: minus four.

Her hand didn't shake.
