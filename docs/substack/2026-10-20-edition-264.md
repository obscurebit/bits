## 📖 Today's Bit

### The Fob

The key fob weighed nothing but opened every door in the building.

Mara clipped it to her carabiner anyway, the plastic click loud in the vestibule where the water-treatment hum lived in the walls. The repurposed dialysis clinic smelled of vinegar and cold epoxy. Vinyl chairs were bolted in rows facing a vending machine that dispensed only electrolyte packets and compression socks, size large. A hand-lettered sign above the coin return read: NO REFUNDS. NO EXCEPTIONS. NO CASH BACK.

She was the intake worker. She read patterns in leftovers: the crumpled tissues stuffed in cup holders, the half-drunk protein shakes sweating on the side tables, the single-use tourniquets rolled tight as cigarettes and printed with batch numbers tracing back to a latex allergy recall the company hadn't acknowledged. She knew which patients flushed their lines at 02:00 and which lied about it in the app. Three missed timestamps auto-flagged a chart for compliance review. Paused supply shipments. Mara had seen the letters. She had also seen the fob override the lock on the supply closet where the unflagged boxes lived.

Mrs. Gant sat in chair four, compression socks bunched at her ankles, a laminated flowchart of difficult vein protocols taped to her walker. She was a mobile phlebotomist. Leased van. Paid per successful stick. Her knees were shot. Her eyes were sharp.

"You're late," Mrs. Gant said.

"Traffic on the bridge."

"The bridge was fine. You stopped for coffee."

Mara didn't argue. She needed the day's cash in hand by six. Her sister needed the rent fiction held for one more week — the story that Mara's hours hadn't been cut, that the overtime was coming back, that the car payment wasn't two weeks behind. The fiction cost twenty dollars a day in performance energy. Mara had seventeen in her wallet.

She scanned Mrs. Gant's wristband. The tablet beeped. Green check. Eligible.

"Left arm today," Mrs. Gant said. "Right's a map of blown valves. And don't dig. I've got a referral for the vascular surgeon next month. If my chart stays clean."

"Your chart's clean."

"Because you make it clean. That's the arrangement."

The arrangement: Mara ignored the missed flushes. Mrs. Gant ignored the fob on Mara's carabiner. The building's access logs showed Mara in the supply closet at 03:14, 03:22, 03:41. The logs didn't show what she took. Gauze. Saline flushes. The good tape. She sold half to Mrs. Gant at cost. The rest she traded to the night nurse for schedule favors. The night nurse traded them to the agency for quiet shifts. The economy ran on stolen sterility.

Mara prepped the site. Chlorhexidine swipe. Tourniquet — batch 7742, the recalled lot — snapped tight. She palpated. Deep. Rolling. She hated the cephalic. But the basilic was a rumor.

"Stick it," Mrs. Gant said. "I've got a dialysis run at eleven. If you miss, I bill the miss to your supervisor."

Mara stuck it. Flash. Dark red. She advanced the catheter, released the tourniquet, taped it down with the good tape. The machine beeped its satisfaction.

"Clean stick," Mrs. Gant said. "Twenty minutes. Don't let the pump alarm."

Mara walked to the nurses' station. The fob clicked against her hip. In the break room, a single paper clip lay bent on the counter — someone had used it to reset a router, maybe, or pick a lock, or clean under a fingernail. It caught the fluorescent light. A tiny wire U. She picked it up, straightened it, bent it again. The metal fatigued. Snapped. She threw the halves in the trash.

Her phone buzzed. Her sister: *Landlord called. Said he's filing tomorrow if he doesn't see something. Anything.*

Mara typed: *Working on it.*

She returned to chair four. Mrs. Gant's bag was full. The pump purred. Mara disconnected, flushed, capped. Applied pressure. Pealed the tourniquet off — batch 7742, rolled tight as a cigarette — and dropped it in the sharps container.

"Same time Thursday," Mrs. Gant said. She held out a folded twenty. Crisp. New. "For the tape. And the silence."

Mara took it. The fob clicked against her keys as she walked Mrs. Gant to the vestibule. The door buzzed open. The night air smelled of exhaust and rain.

"Thursday," Mara said.

"Thursday," Mrs. Gant said. "Bring the good tape. And don't be late. The bridge is never fine."
