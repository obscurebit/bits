### The Notary's Mileage Log

06/14 — $15.00 — Acknowledgment — Mercy General, Room 312

The stamp pad had gone dry again. I twisted the rubber — chipped E in EXPIRES, always the ghost letter unless you cocked your wrist at thirty degrees — and pressed it against the discharge summary. The ink came up patchy, a constellation of faded red. The patient, a woman with oxygen tubing looped behind her ears, watched me with the particular patience of people who have nothing but time. Her daughter stood by the window, scrolling through something on her phone, the blue light washing out her face. "Ma'am, I need you to sign here," I said, tapping the line. "And initial the bottom." The woman's hand trembled. The daughter didn't look up. I've notarized three hundred discharge summaries. I know the rhythm: the apology for handwriting, the squint at the ID photo, the way signers pretend the face across the table matches the laminated card from twenty pounds ago. I pretend not to notice. That's the job.

06/14 — $15.00 — Jurat — County Jail, Intake B

The guard slid the affidavit through the slot. "She's in three," he said. "Don't take all day." The stamp's ghost E haunted me. I twisted, pressed, checked. The woman in Cell 3 had been arrested for retail theft — $47 worth of baby formula and diapers. Her wrists were swollen from the zip ties. "I need you to swear this is true," I said. "Under penalty of perjury." She laughed, a dry hacking sound. "Lady, I'd swear the moon's made of cheese if it got me out of here." Her ID photo showed a different person entirely: round cheeks, a gap between front teeth, a confidence this woman had lost somewhere between booking and now. I stamped anyway. The title company audits my mileage. They don't audit the faces.

06/15 — $15.00 — Copy Certification — Clerk's Recording Room, Vinyl Bench

The timestamp machine jammed. It always jams on Tuesdays. The air tasted of toner and dried highlighter ink. I sat on the cracked vinyl bench, the same bench where I'd signed my own divorce papers three years ago, waiting for the deputy clerk to unjam the roller. A man beside me held a deed. "My brother's name is on this," he said. "But he's been gone two years. Mexico. Or maybe Arizona. Nobody's sure." He wanted a certified copy. The original was in a box somewhere, or burned, or eaten by his brother's ex-wife's dog — the story changed each telling. "Fifteen dollars," I said. "Plus mileage." He paid cash. Crumpled bills. The machine whirred back to life. *THUNK.* The timestamp ate the paper, spat it out certified. The ghost E on my stamp left its specter beside the date. EXPIRES. The man tucked the copy into his wallet like a winning lottery ticket. "This'll work," he said. "The bank won't know the difference."

06/16 — $15.00 — Acknowledgment — Mercy General, Room 312

The daughter met me in the hallway. "She coded at 3:40," she said. "They brought her back. But the doctor says..." She didn't finish. I followed her into the room. The woman was unconscious now, ventilator hissing. The discharge summary sat on the overbed table, signed and initialed in a shaky hand. My stamp, ghost E and all, bled red beside the signature. "I need you to notarize the DNR," the daughter said. "The doctor says we have to decide today." I opened my bag. The stamp pad was dry. I twisted the rubber, cocked my wrist, pressed. Nothing. I licked my thumb, rubbed the pad, tried again. A faint impression. EXPIRES. The ghost E wavered. "Ma'am," I said to the unconscious woman. "Can you hear me? I need you to acknowledge this is your signature." The monitor beeped. The daughter held her mother's hand. "She can hear you," the daughter said. "She always hears." I stamped the DNR. The ghost E left its mark. I wrote in my log: *Signer unresponsive. Daughter witnessed.* The title company would audit the mileage. They never ask about the wrist.
