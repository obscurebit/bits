### The Roller's Union Card

The roller sat at the boundary edge, engine ticking down, a twelve-ton cylinder of cast iron and stubbornness. I'd named it Bessie in '87, back when naming things felt like maintenance. Now it just felt like truth.

"Start it," said Nikhil. Twenty-six, CV stuffed with turf management certificates, mouth shaped like a permanent correction. "The final's Saturday. The square needs to be glass."

"Bessie doesn't do glass on Thursday," I said, wiping grease off my thumbs with a rag that had seen three prime ministers. "She does consolidation. Roots need air."

"Roots need a billiard table. The club paid for a hybrid Bermuda. Fourteen grand a hectare. It doesn't 'need air.' It needs rolling."

The roller's exhaust coughed. Blue smoke, the smell of diesel and old decisions. Bessie had developed opinions three weeks back. First the footpaths—refused to cross the worn tracks where groundsmen had walked for forty years. Just stopped. Engine running, transmission engaged, wheels turning, but the whole machine leaning away like a horse shying at a fence. I'd spent six hours coaxing her across the pavilion path. Next day she wouldn't touch the umpire's route to the crease. Yesterday she backed off the tea lady's shortcut to the scorebox.

"Personification," Nikhil said. "It's a hydraulic pressure sensor. Probably the relief valve sticking. I've booked the dealer for Monday."

"Monday's after the final."

"Then I'll try it myself. The dealer can finish the job Monday."

He climbed the cab steps like he was boarding a spaceship. I watched his clean boots, his pressed polo, the way he adjusted the seat like he was settling a debt. The starter motor whined. The diesel caught. The vibration came up through the frame—wrong. Too smooth. Too willing.

"Forward," Nikhil said into the radio. "Nice and steady."

Bessie moved. Straight at the pavilion path. The worn footpath where generations had walked in spikes and brogues and trainers. The grass there was thin, honest, *earned*.

"Stop her," I said. My voice came out thinner than I meant.

Nikhil didn't hear. Or didn't listen. The roller's front drum kissed the path. The grass flattened. The soil sighed. Something in the machine's hydraulics screamed—a sound like a man inhaling water.

Nikhil killed the engine. Silence rushed back in, full of birds and shame.

"You broke her," I said.

"I *rolled* her. It's a machine, Pete. They don't have feelings."

He climbed down, brushing invisible dust off his hands. The dealer would come Monday. They'd swap the valve, flash the ECU, wipe whatever ghost had grown in the metal. Bessie would roll perfect strips forever. The pitch would be glass. The club would be happy.

I put my hand on the warm flank. The vibration was gone. Just cooling metal, ticking.

"She had a union card," I said. "Local 847. Fifty-two years dues paid."

Nikhil laughed. Short, sharp. "Right. I'll file the grievance."

He walked toward the shed. I stayed. The footpath lay flat behind the roller, a dark stripe across the green. By Saturday the grass would spring back. It always did. But the roller would be fixed. Clean. Obedient.

I lit a cigarette. The smoke drifted over the square, over the paths that weren't paths anymore, over the machine that had remembered something we'd forgotten: that some ground isn't meant to be perfect.

The final would be beautiful. Nobody would notice the difference.

Except me. And the tea lady. And the umpire who'd pause, just a fraction, before crossing that spot on Saturday afternoon.

I exhaled. "Good girl," I whispered to the cooling iron. "Good bloody girl."
