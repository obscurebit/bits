### The Shortcut

"You'll tell her the shortcut saves fifteen minutes. You won't tell her it crosses the rail yard where the 4:10 freight stops for crew change."

Marta didn't look up from the county map spread across the dashboard. Highlighter stripes — yellow for soft soil, orange for sink zones, pink for the three cemeteries that charged extra after three p.m. — caught the late sun through the van's windshield. The engine idled, AC humming against the August heat. Behind the partition, the organist's scales drifted up from the chapel alley, thinning into arpeggios.

"She'll follow the hearse anyway," Marta said. "They all do. Twenty cars in a conga line of grief, running red lights like the dead gave them dispensation."

Her niece, Rosa, sat shotgun with a clipboard and a fresh pack of forms. Twenty-two, nursing degree half-finished, convinced that logistics was love made visible. She'd color-coded the intake sheets: blue for veterans, green for indigent, yellow for the ones whose families would argue about the vault seal.

"Mrs. Kowalski's service," Rosa said, tapping the top form. "St. Adalbert's. Her son insists on the shortcut. Says his father took it in '38. Says it's tradition."

"Her son insists on a lot of things. The mahogany casket. The three-day viewing. The police escort he can't afford." Marta folded the map along the rail yard crease, the laminate cracking. "The 4:10 doesn't care about tradition. It stops. Twenty-two minutes for crew change. I've timed it. Thirty-seven cars stuck behind the gates last Tuesday. The Kowalskis' boy missed his father's committal because he was fourth in line at the crossing."

Rosa's pen hovered. "So we tell him no."

"We tell him the route is under review. County ordinance 14-B. 'Road conditions affecting heavy transport.'" Marta smiled, a small hard thing. "I wrote that ordinance. 1941. After the Milano vault sank eighteen inches in spring thaw and cracked the liner. The family sued the cemetery. The cemetery sued the county. The county sued the gravel supplier. The gravel supplier went bankrupt. I typed the correspondence for all four parties. Sixteen pages. My fingers bled."

"Why didn't you just pave it?"

"Because paving costs money. Ordinances cost ink." She slid the map into the door pocket. "Your aunt the architect of inconvenience. That's what the sextons call me. That's what the monument dealers call me. That's what your mother calls me when she thinks I can't hear her through the kitchen wall."

Rosa's jaw tightened. Her mother — Marta's sister — had called three times this week. *The van needs brakes. The insurance lapsed. Rosa needs a proper job, not driving dead people around.* As if the shuttle wasn't a proper job. As if the county didn't pay on the first and fifteenth. As if Rosa's nursing tuition wasn't coming from Marta's overtime running the indigent route to Potter's Field, where the soil was always soft and the fees were always zero.

"He'll call the commissioner," Rosa said. "Mrs. Kowalski's son. He knows Councilman Velez. They went to seminary together."

"Let him call. Velez owes me." Marta started the van, easing into the alley's rutted drive. "1939. His mother. Indigent. I got her the oak veneer instead of pine. Buried her facing east. He sends a ham every Christmas. Dry as sawdust. I feed it to the stray cats."

Rosa laughed — a sharp bark that surprised them both. "You're terrible."

"I'm useful. There's a difference." Marta signaled, merging onto the boulevard behind the hearse, a black Packard with whitewall tires and a wreath of plastic lilies. The procession formed behind them: a Ford sedan, a Plymouth coupe, a battered truck with a ladder rack. Twenty cars. Maybe twenty-one. The light at 14th and Cedar cycled green, yellow, red. The hearse slipped through. The Ford followed. The Plymouth hesitated, then gunned it.

Marta watched the crossing gates descend in her side mirror, the red lights flashing their mechanical heartbeat. The 4:10 freight rumbled into view, a black snake of boxcars and tankers. It slowed. It stopped. The crew change whistle shrieked.

She checked her watch. Three minutes to the cemetery gates. Twenty-two minutes to the crew change. The shortcut would save fifteen.

"New rule," Marta said, hands loose on the wheel. "We take the long way. 14th to Cedar to Main. Twenty minutes. No trains. No gates. No ordinances."

Rosa wrote it down. Blue ink. Neat block letters. *Route change effective immediately. Per driver discretion.*

"Per driver discretion," Marta repeated. "I like that. Put it on the laminated map. Red highlighter. Right next to the sink zones."

The hearse turned onto Cedar. The Ford turned. The Plymouth turned. One by one, the procession followed, taillights painting the wet asphalt red.

Marta didn't mention the ham. Didn't mention the cats. Didn't mention that she'd already memorized the long way's potholes, the storm drain that flooded at the first rain, the corner where the streetcar tracks ate tires.

She drove. The organist finished the prelude. The chapel doors opened. The work began.
