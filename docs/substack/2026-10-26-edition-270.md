### The Paint Was Dry Before the Sign Went Up

The tree was healthy. The algorithm said remove.

I stood in the bucket at 6:45, coffee cooling in the sap-sticky holder, the resistograph drill warm against my palm. The rubber mallet hung from my belt, unused. Thirty years of sounding trunks and I still brought it, same way I brought the spray can with the clogged nozzle — fluorescent orange REMOVE paint, shaken rhythmically while I walked the drip line. Ritual. Superstition. Call it what you want.

The oak was a birthday tree. Planted 1987, the tag said. Forty years in the public right-of-way, roots cracking the sidewalk, branches weaving through secondary lines. Homeowner: Mrs. Chen. She'd planted it for her son's first birthday. He died at thirty-two, motorcycle, wet leaves on MacArthur Boulevard. She came to every hearing. Sat in the front row with a laminated photo. Cited sentimental value. The review board overrode the algorithm twice.

Third time, they sent me.

"Assessment notes," the foreman said, handing me the work order. "Marginalia. Codes. One sentence fragment per tree. That's the new protocol."

I climbed. The bucket shuddered. Hydraulics whined.

At forty feet, the canopy opened around me — green cathedral, late summer light. The resistograph bit into heartwood. Clean. Solid. No decay pockets. No fungal conks. No carpenter ant galleries. The drill's graph paper spool showed steady resistance, the needle drawing a straight line like a heartbeat at rest.

DBH 42". CRZ intact. Crown class: dominant. Vigor: high.

I wrote in the margins: *Sound. No structural defect. Algorithm flags root-zone conflict only.*

The spray can hissed in my pocket. Cold metal against my thigh.

Mrs. Chen stood on her porch. Blue bathrobe. Gray braid. She held a mug with both hands. Didn't wave. Didn't shout. Just watched.

The politeness was the worst part. Everyone polite. The foreman polite about "liability exposure." The review board polite about "community character." Mrs. Chen polite about "understanding the process." Nobody said: *We are cutting down a living thing because a spreadsheet says its roots might lift a sidewalk slab that hasn't moved in five years.* Nobody said: *The algorithm doesn't know about the boy on the motorcycle.*

I checked the work order again. Tree ID: 7744-Q. Species: Quercus macrocarpa. Priority: 1. Action: REMOVE.

Previous assessor's notes, faded pencil: *Homeowner hostile. Recommend expedited.*

Hostile. She brought cookies to the last hearing. Oatmeal raisin. The board members ate them while voting to save the tree.

I looked at the trunk. Sound. Solid. Forty years of rings. Forty birthdays the boy didn't get.

The paint can nozzle was still clogged. I shook it. Rattled. Shook again.

*One sentence fragment per tree.*

The bucket lowered. Hydraulics sighing. My boots touched pavement.

Mrs. Chen didn't move. Steam rose from her mug.

I pulled the can from my pocket. Shook it one last time. Pressed the nozzle to the bark — not the orange X the protocol demanded, but a small dot, low on the trunk, hidden by shadow and ivy.

*Assessment override: assessor discretion. Tree retained.*

I wrote it in the margin. Truncated. Coded. The one sentence fragment the protocol allowed.

Then I signed the work order. Handed it to the foreman. Climbed into the cab of the bucket truck. Started the engine.

The REMOVE can stayed in my pocket. Still clogged. Still cold.

Mrs. Chen's porch light clicked off.

I drove toward the next address.
