# The Laminator's Hum

The laminator in the senior wing supply closet smelled like burnt hair and melted glue sticks, a scent that clung to your cardigan sleeves until laundry day. It was 1978, and the community center ran on carbon paper, white-out, and the unspoken understanding that Director Henshaw’s agendas would arrive with at least three typos — "Pubic Works" for "Public Works" being the immortal classic — and you simply corrected the copies before the aldermen sat down.

I was twenty-three, Henshaw’s "assistant," which meant I knew where the yellow cardstock for Senior Swim Passes lived, which volunteers showed up hungover on Saturdays (Mrs. Kovic, always Mrs. Kovic), and that the plastic accordion file labeled *VOLUNTEER OF THE MONTH 1998–* was a lie. We were in 1978. The file was empty, its Velcro tab sticky with decade-old glue residue, waiting for a future no one in this building believed they’d see.

Had I checked the closet that Tuesday, the file would not have been where the auditor found it. But I was in the gymnasium, taping "WET FLOOR" signs over the actual wet floor, when Dolores walked back in.

Dolores, who’d left for the county clerk’s office two years prior with a box of her plants and a promise to write. Dolores, who now stood in the doorway of the supply closet holding a Tupperware container of cold casserole — tuna noodle, crushed potato chips on top — and the face of someone who has practiced a conversation in the car.

"You're still here," she said.

"You're not," I said.

The laminator buzzed. A fly threw itself against the buzzing light fixture.

She set the casserole on a shelf beside a stack of discontinued "Senior Swim Pass — Seasonal (Yellow Cardstock Only)" forms. "Henshaw called. Said you're drowning. Said the auditor's Thursday. Said you never ask for help."

"I don't drown."

"Then what's in the file?"

The accordion file. Right. The one I’d started feeding things into three months ago, after the night I misfiled the grant renewal and Henshaw covered for me with a lie about "bureaucratic restructuring." I’d thought: *one thing*. Just the rejection letter. Then the overdue water bill. Then the notice that my night-school credits wouldn't transfer. Things that marked me as someone who failed upward. The file didn't grow thicker, exactly. But the tabs on the front — handwritten in my own blocky script — rearranged themselves. *GRANT* became *GRANT (DENIED)*. *WATER* became *WATER (PAID LATE)*. *CREDITS* became *CREDITS (USELESS)*. The file judged me in my own handwriting.

"Nothing," I said. "Future archives."

Dolores snorted. It sounded the same. "You always were a terrible liar. Open it."

"I can't."

"Because of the tabs? Because they change?"

I froze. The laminator whined, sealing a "Volunteer of the Month — October" photo for Mrs. Kovic, who would absolutely be hungover Saturday.

"You know," I said.

"I *run* the laminator for Parks & Rec bulletin boards now," she said. "I know exactly which paper jams. I know which ink bleeds. And I know that file has been in this closet since 1972, eating the Director's mistakes. Henshaw feeds it his. I fed it mine. You've been feeding it yours."

"The auditor—"

"Wants to see the Volunteer of the Month file. For 'historical continuity.' He'll open it. He'll see your tabs. He'll see mine. He'll see Henshaw's: *AFFAIR (CLERICAL)*, *BUDGET (COOKED)*, *RESIGNATION (DRAFTED 1975)*."

My throat clicked. "You didn't."

"I did. And I'm telling you now because the bargain was: we keep the file, the file keeps us. But the wording changed. The social meaning changed. We thought it was protection. It's evidence."

She pushed the Tupperware toward me. "Eat the casserole. Then we make a new file. Real tabs. *GRANT (REAPPLIED)*. *WATER (AUTO-PAY)*. *CREDITS (COMMUNITY COLLEGE, NIGHTS)*. *ASSISTANT (INTERIM DIRECTOR, PENDING)*."

The laminator finished its cycle. The sealed photo emerged, hot and perfect: Mrs. Kovic, hairnet askew, holding a trash bag like a scepter.

"Since when do you forgive without a confession?" I asked.

"Since I realized the confession was the only thing keeping you here," she said. "Open the file. Burn the tabs. Keep the container."

The auditor arrived Thursday. He opened the accordion file. Found only a single laminated sheet: a handwritten rule, sealed in plastic, dated today.

*NEW RULE: THE ASSISTANT DECIDES WHAT STAYS.*

He frowned. Turned it over. Blank.

"Huh," he said. "Progress."

I signed the sign-in sheet with a steady hand. Dolores, in the back row, winked — a gesture that looked absurd on a woman in a polyester blazer, and therefore exactly right.

The laminator still buzzed. But the file, finally, stayed shut.
