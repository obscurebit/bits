# The Lifeguard's Stamp

The kickboard slipped. Again. I bent to retrieve it from the chlorine-slick floor of the equipment closet, my notary stamp catching on the doorframe—blue ink smearing across white enamel. The first-aid logbook lay open on the metal shelf, its pages warped from humidity. Three affidavits waited in my gym bag, cash-paying clients who needed a witness before the afternoon thunderstorm cleared the deck.

"You're stamping on the clock," Mara said. She leaned against the doorjamb, whistle dangling from her neck, one sneaker propped on the threshold. Her shift-schedule card—yellowed laminate, corners peeled—hung from a clip on her hip. "That's the third one this week."

"Fourth." I capped the stamp. "Mrs. Chen's power of attorney. Her nephew's trying to sell the condo in Flushing while she's in rehab."

Mara snorted. "And the schedule card?"

I glanced at the laminate. Five names printed in block letters. Four remained. Every time I used it to verify an alibi—covering Diaz's unauthorized break last Tuesday, swapping my Sunday for her anniversary—the card lost a name. Not crossed out. *Gone*. The plastic simply forgot the ink had ever been there.

"Down to four," I said. "Diaz, me, you, and the new kid. Torres."

"Torres doesn't count. He's seasonal." Mara pushed off the doorframe. "Look, the rotation log says I was on stand three from two to four. The log *also* says you covered my break at three-fifteen. Both can't be true."

"Both are true in the log."

"Which is the problem." She stepped closer, close enough that I smelled sunscreen and pool chemical beneath the damp wool smell of the closet. "The district auditor comes next month. They'll compare the log to the card. Four names on the card, five guards on the roster. Someone's getting fired."

"Then don't let them compare."

Mara laughed, short and sharp. "You're the notary. You notarize the lie, I live it. That's the arrangement."

"Since when?"

"Since you started charging seventy-five dollars a stamp and I started covering your Saturdays so you could take your mother to dialysis." She tapped the schedule card. "Every name that disappears is a favor I can't cash in. Diaz is already talking about quitting. Torres won't last the summer. That leaves you and me, and I'm not covering your ass when the card goes blank."

The thunderstorm hit then—rain hammering the pool deck, thunder shaking the cinderblock walls. Through the propped door, I watched parents herd children toward the parking lot, umbrellas blooming like toxic flowers. The pool surface churned, lanes erased.

"My mother's appointment moved to Thursday," I said.

"Thursday's my sister's wedding."

"I know."

Mara was quiet. The logbook's pages rippled as a gust caught the door. "You'll sign the affidavit saying I was on stand three. You'll stamp it. The card loses another name. Then what?"

"Then I stop taking clients. Thursday through Sunday. I cover your wedding, you cover my mother. We ride out the audit on borrowed time."

"Borrowed from who?"

"From the version of us that still has names on the card."

She studied me—lifeguard, notary, daughter, debtor. The whistle at her chest caught a draft. "You're terrible at this job."

"I'm terrible at *leaving* this job."

"Same thing."

Mara unclipped the schedule card. Held it out. The laminate caught the fluorescent light, yellow as old bruises. Four names. Three, if I signed. Two, if the auditor came before Torres quit.

I took the card. My thumb brushed the empty space where a fifth name had lived—*Garcia*, maybe, or *Park*, someone who'd needed a break bad enough to cost them their existence on the roster.

"Thursday," I said. "I'll bring a gift. Nothing expensive."

"Bring the stamp," Mara said. "I'll need you to notarize the marriage license. My sister's fiancé lost his birth certificate in a move. Bureaucracy's a bitch."

The rain slowed. On the deck, the first parents were returning, shaking out towels, checking watches. The pool water settled, lanes reasserting themselves.

I capped the stamp again. Blue ink stained my thumb. The kickboard still lay at my feet, dented from years of small bodies learning to float.

"Thursday," I repeated. "Stand three. Two to four."

Mara clipped the card back to her hip. "Two to four. Don't be late."

She left the door propped. The logbook sat open, its pages drying. I bent for the kickboard—my stamp catching the doorframe again, another blue smear on white enamel—and thought about how many names a schedule could lose before the pool simply closed.
