# The Recurring Forty-Three

The cake order came in every Tuesday at 4:17 p.m. sharp. A white sheet cake, two layers, buttercream roses piped around the border. The message in blue gel: *Happy 43rd, Elias.* The customer, a man named Corbin who wore a windbreaker the color of bruised plums, paid cash and never waited for a receipt.

Mara, the apprentice, noticed the anomaly on her third cycle. She was scraping the bowl of yesterday’s batter — vanilla, sour cream, a pinch of cardamom the head baker, Luis, insisted on — when she found the rose. A single buttercream rose, perfect and stiff, wedged deep in the fresh batter like a fossil. She picked it out with her fingertip. It tasted of sugar and the specific, faintly metallic tang of the blue dye batch from Tuesday past.

She showed Luis. He didn't look up from scaling flour. "Put it in the scrap bin. Don't let the health inspector see."

"But it's yesterday's rose. From the cake Corbin picked up at 5:00."

Luis scraped his bench knife across the steel. "Corbin orders the cake. We bake the cake. Corbin pays. The bakery stays open. The rose goes in the bin."

The next Tuesday, Mara watched the cooler. Corbin arrived at 4:17. He took the box. He did not open it. He left. At 6:00, Luis sent her to scrape the bowl for the next day's batter. There it was again. Two roses this time. Blue. Perfect.

She started saving them. A Tupperware container hidden behind the fifty-pound sack of bread flour. By October, she had forty-three roses. She arranged them on a sheet of parchment: a calendar of Tuesdays.

On the forty-fourth Tuesday, Corbin didn't come.

The shop fell quiet in a way that felt like a held breath. Luis baked the cake anyway. "Order's in the book," he said. He iced it. He piped the border. He wrote *Happy 43rd, Elias* in blue gel. He set the box on the counter.

Mara stared at it. The roses on the border were fresh, crisp. The ones in her container had softened, the dye bleeding into the buttercream, turning them a bruised, vascular purple.

At 7:00 p.m., the door chimed. It was not Corbin. It was a woman. Gray hair cut in a sharp bob. A coat that had seen better decades. She asked for Corbin's order.

Mara looked at Luis. Luis looked at the woman. The woman looked at the cake.

"Elias," she said. "He turned forty-three thirty years ago. He stayed forty-three for a long time. Leukemia. The hospice volunteers, they did a party every year. In the hospital. Same cake. Same age." She tapped the box. "Corbin was his nurse. He kept ordering it after. Every Tuesday. Said it helped him remember the shape of the boy."

She paid cash. She took the box. She did not ask for a receipt.

Mara went to the back. She opened her Tupperware. Forty-three purple roses. She scraped them, one by one, into the fresh vanilla batter for Wednesday's crumb cake. She mixed it slow. She watched the purple bleed into the yellow, turning the whole bowl a strange, ordinary lavender.
