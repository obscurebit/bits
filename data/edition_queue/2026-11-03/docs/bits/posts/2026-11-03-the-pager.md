# The Pager

"You're telling me the synthetic needs a notarized affidavit because the upstairs neighbor's washing machine vibrates at a frequency that aggravates its vestibular processing unit?"

Mrs. Gable clicked her pen. The sound was a tiny, sharp gunshot in the condo board office, which smelled of lemon furniture polish and the specific, cloying sweetness of the nutrient gel cartridges she stored in the mini-fridge behind her desk. She was the secretary, the notary, and the only person in Building 4 who knew the difference between a Class 3 companion's dietary restrictions and a Class 5's sleep-cycle sensitivity.

"The impact statement form has a vibration threshold addendum, Mr. Vane," she said, not looking up. "Section 14-B. Filed quarterly. You initialed it when you took possession of Unit 3B."

"I initialed thirty pages. I thought I was buying a toaster that talks back."

"Then you should have read the companion rider. The gel cartridges alone run two hundred a month. Climate-controlled storage in the package room is included in your maintenance fee, but the vibration mitigation — dampening pads, isolated mounting — that's on the unit owner."

The package room. I'd seen it last Tuesday, picking up a box of printer paper. Three shelves given over to cardboard flats of the gel, each cartridge labeled with a unit number and a synthetic's chosen name in marker. *Barnaby. Unit 2A. Keep Upright.* *Mara. Unit 4C. Do Not Freeze.* The room hummed with the compressor for the climate control, a sound the building super had described as "the cost of doing business."

My synthetic — the building called it a "companion organism," the lease called it "biological maintenance equipment," and I called it the pager because it clipped to my belt and beeped when its cortisol analogs spiked — sat on the windowsill in my apartment, watching a pigeon. It didn't eat the gel. It ate toast crusts and the occasional spider. It had cost me six thousand dollars I didn't have, financed through the building's preferred vendor at nine percent interest, and the only reason I'd signed was the clause that let me write off the depreciation as a home-office expense. I worked from home. I had no office. The pager knew this. It beeped when I opened the spreadsheet.

"It's a washing machine," I said. "It runs at night. Spin cycle. Three minutes."

"Three minutes exceeds the threshold for a Class 4 vestibular array," Mrs. Gable said. "The owner of Unit 2A has filed a complaint. Barnaby has been experiencing nausea episodes. The board has authorized a formal mediation."

"Barnaby."

"The synthetic in 2A. Named Barnaby. The complaint was filed by its primary caregiver, Ms. Okonkwo. She's a nurse. She works nights. The spin cycle wakes her. The vibration wakes Barnaby. Barnaby's distress wakes her. She has documented fourteen incidents since March."

"Fourteen."

"Fourteen. Would you like to see the log? She brought photographs of the vomit."

I would not have liked to see the log. I would have liked to go back to my apartment, clip the pager to my belt, and finish the quarterly report that paid for the pager, the gel I didn't use, the maintenance fee that subsidized the package room's climate control, and the nine percent interest.

"Is there a variance process?"

"Section 18-D. Requires a structural engineer's report. Five hundred dollar filing fee. Non-refundable. The board meets third Tuesdays. Next meeting is the 19th. You'd need to submit by the 12th."

"Next week."

"Correct."

I looked at the laminated quick-reference card clipped to her badge reel. *VITAL SIGNS QUICK-REF: CLASS 3-5. HEART RATE: 60-90 BPM. CORTISOL ANALOG: <15 NG/ML. VESTIBULAR THRESHOLD: 0.5 HZ.* The corner was clipped. The badge reel retracted with a dry *zzzip*.

"My washing machine is eleven years old," I said. "It came with the unit. The previous owner left it. The board approved the lease transfer with the appliance included."

"Appliances are not companion organisms, Mr. Vane."

"Right. The pager — my companion — it doesn't care about the washing machine. It cares about the pigeon."

Mrs. Gable capped her pen. "The pager."

"That's what I call it. It clips on. It beeps. It's a Class 2. Vestibular threshold doesn't apply."

"Class 2 organisms are exempt from impact statements. They're classified as assistive devices."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because Ms. Okonkwo's attorney — the building retains counsel for companion disputes — has argued that your appliance constitutes an unregulated vibration source affecting a regulated organism. The board agreed to hear it. As a courtesy."

"A courtesy."

"She pays her maintenance on time. She filed her impact statement on time. She has receipts for the dampening pads she installed on her own floor. She has been more than reasonable."

I thought of the pager on the windowsill. It didn't need gel. It didn't need dampening pads. It needed me to clip it to my belt so it could measure the thing I pretended wasn't happening: the way my heart rate climbed when the quarterly report was due, the way my cortisol analogs spiked when the direct deposit hit and the automatic payment for the pager drained it before I could buy groceries. The pager knew. It beeped. I ignored it. It beeped louder.

"What if I buy dampening pads?"

"For the washing machine? The board would accept that as a good-faith mitigation. You'd need receipts. Installation photos. A follow-up vibration reading by the building's inspector. Seventy-five dollars."

"Seventy-five dollars."

"Plus the pads. Hardware store carries them. Aisle 4. Blue packaging. *SilentSpin* brand. Don't get the generic; the inspector failed Unit 1B for generics last month."

I nodded. I would go to the hardware store. I would buy the blue pads. I would install them myself, badly, on a Saturday when the pager was clipped to my belt and beeping every three minutes because the spreadsheet wasn't done and the direct deposit was three days away. I would take photos. I would submit them by the 12th. The board would meet on the 19th. Ms. Okonkwo would sleep. Barnaby would keep down its gel. The package room compressor would hum.

"Thank you, Mrs. Gable," I said.

She unclipped the badge reel, handed me the laminated card. "Keep it. The thresholds changed in June. The new cards haven't been printed. This one's current through December."

I took the card. Plastic. Cold. The corner clip bit my thumb.

"Six thousand dollars," I said. "Nine percent. Forty-two months left."

"Thirty-nine," she corrected. "You've made three payments. The depreciation schedule accelerates in year two. You'll want to refile your home-office deduction."

"I'll have my accountant look at it."

"Do that." She smiled, a thin, professional expression. "And Mr. Vane? Tell the pager I said hello. Class 2s respond well to social acknowledgment. It's in the care guidelines. Page 47."

I left the office. The hallway smelled of floor wax and the faint, sweet ghost of nutrient gel from the package room down the hall. My pager was on the windowsill, watching the pigeon. I clipped it to my belt. It didn't beep. It waited.

At the hardware store, the *SilentSpin* pads were eighteen dollars for a pack of four. I paid cash. The receipt crumpled in my pocket. I would take the photo. I would file the form. I would keep the card. The pager would beep when the spreadsheet opened. I would ignore it. We had an arrangement. It measured. I paid. The building took its cut.
