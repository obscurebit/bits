# The Penny Lock

Mara arrived at the facilities review with a thermos of cold coffee and a spreadsheet nobody had asked for. The meeting room smelled of dry-erase marker and the particular dust of Victorian taxidermy — arsenic-treated, the placards admitted, but safe behind glass. She sat in the back row, boots still damp from the service corridor's broken drain, and watched the new compliance director click through slides about "universal accessibility pathways."

"Doorway clearance," the director said, tapping a schematic. "Minimum 230 millimeters. Non-negotiable."

Mara's laser meter, cracked lens and all, sat heavy in her pocket. She knew the drift: 230 read 228. 110 read 112. She corrected mentally, never officially. The union contract required two witnesses for any environmental disturbance report. The diorama dust samples had sat in evidence bags for three weeks while the second signatory recovered from shingles.

"Ma'am?" The director's gaze landed on her badge. "Facilities compliance?"

"Measurements," Mara said. "Actual ones."

A murmur. The director smiled the smile of people who benefit from decisions they don't make. "We have contractors for that. Certified. Insured."

"Your contractors measured the main hall at 232," Mara said. "My meter reads 228. The drift is known. The door frame hasn't moved since 1852."

"1852," the director repeated. "Charming. But we work to current code."

Mara's phone buzzed. A text from her sister: *Mom's asking about the penny lock again.*

The penny lock. Brass, Victorian, salvaged from a demolished railway station and installed on the museum's only gender-neutral toilet as a "heritage feature." Insert a penny, the bolt throws. No penny, no entry. The facilities team had argued for a modern lock. The heritage board had won. Mara's mother, eighty-two, rheumatoid, visited every Tuesday. She never carried change. Mara kept a jar of pennies in her desk drawer, a debt she calculated in minutes of her mother's dignity.

"The service corridor," Mara said, standing. "The shortcut wheelchair users actually take. The floor slopes toward a drain that hasn't worked since the seventies. The clearance there is 215. Your contractors measured the front door."

The room went quiet. The director's smile thinned. "That corridor isn't on the accessibility map."

"Because you didn't ask the people who use it."

Mara left before the vote. She walked the service corridor, laser meter clicking against her thigh. 228. 230. 229. The drift was predictable. The slope was measurable. The drain would need a pump, not a spreadsheet.

Her phone buzzed again. *She's at the door. No pennies in her purse.*

Mara checked her pocket. Three pennies. Four minutes to the front desk. The meeting would end in twenty. The director would sign off on the front door clearance. The service corridor would stay off the map. Her mother would wait, or Mara would pay.

She turned toward the main entrance, counting steps. The penny lock clicked in her mind — a mechanism, a debt, a calculation she'd never forgive herself for making.
