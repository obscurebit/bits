# The Boat That Talked Back

Old Charlie found it in the western red cedar, two planks up from the keel. He'd been running his palm along the grain, checking for twist, when the wood warmed under his hand like a stove eye. He stopped. Listened. The grain didn't speak words — it answered questions.

"Will the epoxy hold on the starboard chine?" The grain ran smooth and straight. Yes.

"Should I scarf the garboard at eighteen inches or twenty-two?" A knot swirled, dark and decisive. Eighteen.

Charlie told his apprentice, a kid named Miguel who'd been with him three years and still held his chisel wrong, to grab a beer and sit quiet. Miguel sat. Charlie asked the hull about the bronze fastenings versus silicon bronze, about the fairing compound ratio, about whether the owner'd notice if he shorted the varnish coats. The boat answered every one.

Then the owner showed up. Rick. Fifty-ish, divorce settlement money burning a hole in his dock lines, wanted a twenty-eight foot cutter finished by June. He stood there in his Sperrys and pressed polo, smelling like expensive gin and bad decisions.

"Charlie. She'll carry me to the Marquesas, right? Single-handed?"

Charlie's hand found the plank. The grain went dead flat. No figure, no runout, just straight lines marching to the transom.

"She'll carry you," Charlie said.

Rick beamed. "Knew it. Knew she had it in her. My ex — she said I'd never make the crossing. Said I was buying a fantasy to avoid the settlement meeting tomorrow."

The grain stayed flat.

"She also said — " Rick laughed, sharp. "She said the only thing I've ever finished is a bottle. That I'd abandon this boat same as I abandoned the marriage counseling. The therapy. The stepdaughter's graduation."

Charlie's fingers traced the grain. It didn't warm. Didn't answer. Just sat there, western red cedar doing what western red cedar does: exist.

"Rick," Charlie said. "The varnish. You want eight coats or twelve?"

"Twelve. Obviously twelve. I'm not a savage."

"Twelve coats takes three weeks. You got the settlement meeting tomorrow."

Rick's face moved through calculations. "I'll hire a detailer. Pay whatever. The boat's ready, right? You said she'll carry me."

"I said she'll carry you. Didn't say you'd be on her when she leaves."

Rick stared. The yard sounds kept happening — Miguel's radio, a compressor cycling, gulls fighting over a crab pot. Charlie's palm stayed on the plank.

"Your wife," Charlie said. "She ask you to the graduation?"

"She sent the invitation. I didn't RSVP."

"She ask you to the settlement?"

"Tomorrow. Ten a.m. Her lawyer's office."

"The boat answers questions about the boat, Rick. Not about you."

Rick's jaw worked. He looked at the hull. Looked at Charlie. Looked at his watch — a thing that cost more than Miguel's truck.

"Ten a.m.," Rick said. "I'll be there."

He walked off without shaking hands. Charlie watched him get in his Audi, watched the brake lights paint the shed red.

Miguel cracked his beer. "So. The wood. It actually — "

"Wood doesn't talk, mijo. Wood remembers. Big difference."

"Right. Right. So what'd it remember?"

Charlie pulled his hand away. The grain was just grain again — beautiful, ordinary, silent.

"It remembered I still got a daughter in Tucson who doesn't return my calls. And that I knew that before I put my hand on the plank."

He picked up his block plane. "Eight coats on the varnish, Miguel. Rick's paying for twelve, but eight's what the boat needs. Don't tell him different."

Miguel nodded, already reaching for his chisel — still holding it wrong, but he'd learn. They all learned eventually.
