# The Badge

The badge clipped to her lanyard with a sound like a staple gun. Marta pressed it flat against her chest, the plastic cold through her cardigan. The photo showed a woman with darker hair and a jawline that hadn't yet softened into jowls. Ten years ago. Fifteen pounds ago. A different economy.

"You're early," the guard said. He didn't look up from his crossword. "Shift doesn't start for twelve minutes."

"Twelve minutes is twelve minutes of rent," Marta said.

The guard grunted. He knew. Everyone knew. The building ran on a schedule printed in 2019 and never updated. The elevators still chimed for floors that had been sealed since the water main burst. The HVAC cycled on a timer calibrated for three hundred bodies; tonight there would be twelve.

Marta walked the corridor to the witness-prep room. Windowless. The fluorescent tubes flickered in rhythm with the elevator relays three floors down — *buzz-click, buzz-click* — a frequency that made her molars ache. She'd complained once. The facilities manager had laughed and said the frequency was "within operational parameters."

The room smelled of dry-erase marker and old coffee. A rubber date stamp sat on the metal desk, its band cracked, the ink pad crusted to gray felt. You had to breathe on it to wake the color. Forty-eight hours exactly. That was the unwritten rule. Two days' grace for memory to settle into its official shape.

She sat. Waited. The badge weighed against her sternum.

The door opened. A woman entered — mid-thirties, sensible shoes, a folder clutched against her chest like a shield. Maria Alvarez. Domestic violence docket. Her husband had broken her orbital bone last November. The trial was Thursday.

"Ms. Alvarez," Marta said. "I'm your coordinator."

Maria nodded. Her eyes tracked to the badge. *MARTA RIVAS — SENIOR WITNESS COORDINATOR — ACCESS LEVEL 3.* The badge recorded intent. Not action. Intent. If Maria hesitated at the door, the badge logged *willingness to testify.* If she cried, it logged *emotional engagement.* If she left the building, it logged *temporary withdrawal.* The system didn't care what happened. Only what was meant to happen.

"Let's begin," Marta said.

She opened the folder. The police report. Medical photos. The neighbor's statement — *Mrs. Kowalski from 3B, who heard everything and called 911 and then called again when the dispatcher put her on hold.* Mrs. Kowalski had testified at the preliminary hearing. Her badge had logged *civic duty fulfilled.* Two weeks later, her landlord raised her rent eight percent. Coincidence. Everything was coincidence.

"Tell me what happened," Marta said.

Maria spoke. Her voice was flat. Rehearsed. Marta's red pencil moved — *hesitation at 'he grabbed me,' correction at 'I fell,' pause before 'I didn't want to.'* The stenographer's marks between the lines. The calibration.

"Good," Marta said. "Now tell it again. Slower. The jury trusts rain more than people. The weather log shows drizzle that night. You'll say you heard it on the roof."

Maria's throat moved. "It wasn't raining."

"The log says it was."

"I was there."

"The log is what the jury sees." Marta tapped the date stamp. "Forty-eight hours. That's how long it takes for truth to become evidence."

Maria's hands tightened on the folder. "You're asking me to lie."

"I'm asking you to survive." Marta leaned forward. The badge clicked against the desk. "The system doesn't protect women, Maria. It processes them. Your husband's badge logs *gainful employment* and *community ties.* Yours logs *instability* and *prior police contact.* The only thing that changes the output is the input. You give them the rain, they give you the conviction."

"And if I won't?"

"Then he walks. And you know what happens after."

Maria stared at the badge. The plastic. The photo. The access level.

"Mrs. Kowalski," Maria said. "She's the one who called. Twice. Her badge — "

"Logs *good neighbor,*" Marta said. "Eight percent rent increase. Her landlord's cousin sits on the housing board. The badge doesn't record that part."

Silence. The fluorescent tubes *buzz-clicked.* The elevator relays thumped somewhere below.

Maria opened the folder. Pulled out a photograph. Held it out.

"This is my daughter," she said. "She's six. She sleeps with a baseball bat under her pillow. Her badge logs *school attendance* and *well-child visits.* It doesn't log the bat."

Marta took the photo. A child with dark curls and a missing front tooth. Smiling at a birthday cake.

"She made a wish," Maria said. "She told me what it was. She said, 'I wish the badge would break.'"

Marta's fingers tightened on the photograph. The date stamp sat beside her elbow, its cracked band, its crust of ink. Forty-eight hours. Two days to turn a lie into a record. Two days to turn a woman into a witness.

She picked up the stamp. Breathed on the pad. The ink woke, dark and thick.

"Thursday," Marta said. "Nine a.m. Courtroom 4C. You'll hear the rain on the roof."

Maria didn't move. "And if the badge breaks?"

Marta pressed the stamp to the affidavit. *THURSDAY. 9:00 AM. COURTROOM 4C.* The ink bled into the paper fibers.

"Then we'll finally have to remember for ourselves."
