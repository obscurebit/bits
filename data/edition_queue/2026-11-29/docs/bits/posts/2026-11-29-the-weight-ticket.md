# The Weight Ticket

The scale ticket prints before the truck stops rolling. Carbon copy, three-part, the middle sheet pink as a blush. Driver hands it through the window without looking. His knuckles are raw from the steering wheel, the same two spots every winter.

Mara tears the pink copy, files the white, tosses the yellow into the bin that never gets emptied. Forty-seven tickets today. Forty-six yesterday. The number means nothing until it means everything.

Her booth smells of floor wax and burnt coffee from the vending machine outside. The heated shelter at the park-and-ride hums with the light-rail's arrival, a vibration through the soles of her boots. She feels the weight of each truck before she reads it. A pressure in her fingertips, calibrated by twenty-three years of gravel, sand, asphalt, the occasional load of demolition debris someone swore was clean fill.

The new manager, Hendricks, renovated the booth last month. Plexiglas partition. Electronic keypad. Removed the stool. "Ergonomic assessment," he called it. Mara stands now. Her left knee locks after three hours. She requested a chair. He filed the request under "personnel comfort items — non-essential."

Her sister calls at 4:17 PM. The phone cord stretches across the booth, tangling with the ticket printer.

"Mom's asking about the house again," her sister says. "The listing. She wants to know if the photos are recent."

Mara tears a ticket. Truck 842. Gravel. 28.4 tons. "They're recent."

"She thinks you're coming Sunday. For the open house."

"I'm working Sunday."

"You're always working." A pause. "She baked the coffee cake. The one with the streusel you pick off first."

The ticket printer chatters. Truck 911. Asphalt. 31.2 tons. Mara's fingers find the folded orange surveyor's flag in her coat sleeve. She uses them to mark the potholes on the access road the city crew keeps forgetting. Three flags this month. The crew hasn't come.

"Tell her I'll call," Mara says.

"She knows you won't. She just needs to hear you say you might."

The phone clicks. Mara files the pink copy. The weight tickets are the only things that balance.

At 6:03 PM, a truck she doesn't recognize pulls up. No company logo. Rust eating the wheel wells. The driver doesn't roll down the window. Just holds a folded paper against the glass.

Mara walks around. The night air presses cold against her face. She reads the paper through the dirty glass: a maintenance tag, yellowed, taped to the dashboard. *EXEMPT — MUNICIPAL USE ONLY.* The date stamp reads 1987.

She knows this truck. It hasn't crossed her scale in eleven years. The driver's face is familiar in the way a scar is familiar — you remember the accident, not the skin.

He rolls the window down two inches. "Ticket."

"Municipal exempt."

"Scale reads regardless." Her voice is flat. The rule posted on the booth wall: *ALL VEHICLES STOP. ALL LOADS WEIGH. NO EXCEPTIONS.* Hendricks put it up last week. Laminated. The corners are already curling.

The driver's hand appears. A folded bill. Twenty dollars. "Off the books. Like always."

Mara looks at the bill. At the maintenance tag. At the driver's wrist, where a tattoo peeks from his sleeve — a date, she thinks, or coordinates. She feels the weight of the truck without the scale. Heavy. Heavier than asphalt. Heavier than gravel.

Her sister's voice: *She just needs to hear you say you might.*

Mara takes the twenty. Tucks it into her coat pocket, next to the orange flag. She tears a blank ticket from the emergency pad, writes *MUNICIPAL — 0.00* in ballpoint, hands it through the gap.

The driver nods. Rolls up the window. Drives onto the access road, hitting the first pothole hard.

Mara watches the taillights. The scale reads zero. The ticket reads zero. The twenty burns warm against her ribs through the wool.

She files the pink copy anyway. Forty-eight tickets. The number balances.

Her phone rings again. She lets it ring. The booth hums. The light-rail shudders past. Somewhere, a crossing guard notices a dog limping before its owner does. The vending machine hums. The coffee burns.

Mara tears the next blank ticket. Writes her name. *MARA — 0.00*. Files it.
