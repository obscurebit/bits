# The Wire Spool

Mrs. Gable asked us to fix the loop again, which meant she stood at the bottom of the choir loft stairs holding a casserole dish wrapped in foil while the amplifier hummed its sick note. She didn't ask if we had time. She asked if the Lord would mind a few more minutes of patience, which was her way of saying the vestry had voted to cut the repair budget to zero and the organist was the only line item left.

We being me, Marlene, and the mint tin. The tin held twenty feet of 22-gauge magnet wire stripped from the church's 1973 intercom system, the kind with the bakelite handsets that weighed a pound each. I'd harvested it the week the trustees voted to rip the whole system out for a wireless PA that never arrived. The wire lives in an Altoids tin because the Altoids tin fits in the pocket of my cardigan. The cardigan lives in the loft because the loft is where the loop amplifier sits, bolted to a plywood shelf that smells like floor wax and dried hydrangeas from Easter 1994.

The loop is a perimeter antenna. It runs under the carpet, up the walls, across the ceiling joists — a copper necklace for the sanctuary. When it works, the hearing aids in pews three through twelve pick up the organ, the pastor, the soloist. When it fails, Mrs. Gable announces technical difficulties from the pulpit and the congregation nods like patience is a spiritual discipline instead of a broken solder joint.

Adjust gain. Test left pew. Nod to the usher.

The first timeline: Sunday morning, 1998. I splice a fresh section of magnet wire into the feed point behind the organ console. The insulation is brittle. It cracks under my fingernails. Each repair steals six inches from the spool. Twenty feet becomes nineteen becomes eighteen. The tin gets lighter. The loop sings clearer. Mrs. Gable brings tuna noodle casserole. The pastor smiles the smile that means thank you for making us look competent.

The second timeline: Wednesday night, 1999. The vestry installs the wireless PA. It cost three thousand dollars. It feeds back during the offertory. The pastor taps the microphone. The congregation winces. Mrs. Gable stands at the bottom of the stairs with an empty casserole dish. She doesn't say the Lord's patience. She says the vestry wants the loop decommissioned. Wireless is modern. The loop is a liability. The insurance man said so.

I hold the mint tin. Eight feet of wire left. The loop still works because I've been stealing from the intercom's corpse for eighteen months. Each splice is a subtraction. The wire doesn't grow back.

The wireless PA fails again on Easter 2000. The pastor's microphone cuts out mid-sermon. Mrs. Gable walks to the organ, climbs the narrow stairs, finds me at the amplifier with my soldering iron. She carries no casserole. She carries the vestry's letter on church letterhead.

Decommission by June. Budget reallocated. Thank you for your service.

I look at the tin. Three feet. The loop hums steady. The hearing aids in pews three through twelve are the only things in this building that never lie.

Mrs. Gable reads the letter again. Her fingers tighten on the paper. She doesn't say technical difficulties. She doesn't say the Lord's patience. She says, "How much wire you got left?"

Three feet.

She nods. "Next Sunday's the confirmation class. Twelve kids. Three wear aids."

I splice the last three feet into the feed point on Pentecost. The tin is empty. The loop sings. The wireless PA feeds back. The pastor taps the microphone. Mrs. Gable stands in the pulpit and doesn't announce technical difficulties. She announces the hymn number. The congregation sings. The hearing aids pick up every note.

I walk down the narrow stairs past the dried hydrangeas. The mint tin rattles in my pocket, empty as a promise kept.
