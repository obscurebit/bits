# The Valve Exerciser's Almanac

You apply penetrating oil. You wait twenty minutes. You do not force it.

I forced it. I always force it.

The hydrant cap surrendered with a sound like a joint popping, and a geyser of rust-brown water hit the windshield of the city manager's leased sedan. He was sitting in it. Reading a bond prospectus. He did not move, just turned a page, the water sheeting over the glass.

"Log it," said Reyes, leaning on his shovel. He has seniority. He gets the dry hydrants on Maple and Elm — clean threads, easy turns, the kind where the cap spins off like a jar of pickles. I get the wet ones. The ones with spider egg sacs in the threads. The ones that lie about their pressure.

"Log what?" I asked. "That you sent the new guy to Hydrant 407 knowing the packing gland was shot?"

Reyes smiled. The kind that doesn't touch his eyes. "Log the exercise. Twenty turns. Full flow. No anomalies."

The logbook lives in a zippered Cordura pouch bolted to the truck dash. Green cover, pages yellowed at the edges. Every entry subtracts something. Not water. Not pressure. *Options.* Each solved hydrant removes a reason for me to be here tomorrow.

I uncapped the pen. The ink had separated. I shook it, the little ball bearing clicking against the glass like a dice in a cup.

"Forty-seven," I wrote. "Hydrant 407. Twenty turns. Full flow. No anomalies."

Reyes watched me write. "You're learning."

"I'm lying."

"We all lie. The system requires it." He tapped the logbook. "That book's older than you. Older than me. It knew the pipes when they were new. It remembers the men who laid them. It subtracts a little patience every time we sign our names."

The city manager's sedan wipers thumped a slow, embarrassed rhythm. He rolled down the window an inch. "Is this water potable?"

"Flushing main," I said. "Not for drinking."

He nodded, rolled it up. Went back to his prospectus. He didn't ask my name. Didn't ask if I was okay. He was being judged by people who didn't understand the work — the bond rating agency, probably — and he needed the flush to happen on schedule. My wet boots, my gasoline-smelling wrench, my wrist that clicks on turn seventeen: not his problem.

Reyes handed me the pentagon wrench. The friction tape wrapped its handle smelled like my grandfather's garage. Worn oval at the corners from generations of hands. "Next one's yours too. 412. Corner of Pine and the old rail cut."

"412's a wet one."

"All yours."

I walked. The logbook stayed in the truck. That was the mistake — thinking the book was the record. The record is the body. The click in the wrist. The mental map of which hydrants lie. 412 doesn't lie. 412 *screams*. Spider nests in the bonnet. Water hammer risk that can snap a femur if you're standing wrong.

I set up. Penetrating oil. Twenty minutes. The condensation inside the riser pipe rained on my hard hat — warm, humid, the only warm thing in February. The ladder rang with hammer strikes from the crew two blocks over, replacing a service line.

I didn't wait twenty minutes. I waited twelve. I forced it.

The cap came free. The water came up clean. Clear as a promise.

Reyes drove up in the truck, window down. "Log says twenty turns."

"I did twenty."

"Book says forty-seven for 407. Forty-eight for 412. That's ninety-five turns in an hour." He shook his head. "Kid, the book subtracts. You add. That's not how it works."

I looked at my hands. The wrench. The tape worn smooth where my thumb rides.

"Retirement's mandatory at sixty," Reyes said. "Or when the book says you're done. Whichever comes first."

"I'm thirty-two."

"The book doesn't care about age. It cares about *turns*." He gestured at the pouch. "You've got maybe two hundred left in you. Three hundred if you stop forcing."

The city manager's sedan was gone. The prospectus, the bond rating, the schedule — all driven away clean.

"Log it," Reyes said. "Forty-eight turns. Full flow. No anomalies."

I took the pen. The ink flowed. I wrote the lie.

"Thanks for the dry one," I said.

Reyes laughed, started the truck. "412's wet as a sponge. You just don't feel it anymore."

He drove off. The wiper fluid nozzles on his side sprayed a perfect arc onto the windshield — clear, blue, smelling of methanol and winter.

I stood in the drying street, water steaming off my boots, and realized I'd stopped counting turns in my wrist three hydrants ago. The book knew. Reyes knew. The hydrants knew.

Only me. Still writing.
