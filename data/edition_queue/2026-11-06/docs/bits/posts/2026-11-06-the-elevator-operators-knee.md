# The Elevator Operator's Knee

You feel the building settle through the soles of your shoes. The hydraulic ram groans, a low shudder traveling up the steel grating, into your left knee — click — and out through the palm clamped on the lever. Fourth floor. Millinery. Mrs. Vane's hat pins catch the cage light as she steps off, her purse snapping shut with the sound of a trap closing.

"Level it proper, Eddie," she says, not looking back. "My bonus depends on sandwiches."

The floor-indicator arrow sticks between 4 and 5. You tap the glass with your thumbnail — *tap, tap, tap* — the rhythm you've worn into the brass. The arrow shudders free, settling on 4. The cage breathes. Your knee clicks again, louder this time.

Third floor. Men's furnishings. Mr. Haskins boards with his clipboard, his pencil, his silence. He marks something. You don't ask. The floor-sheet on your clipboard — chained to the gate latch, edges frayed — already shows today's deductions: seventeen cents for Tuesday's hesitation at 2, twenty-two for Thursday's wobble at 5. The building knows. The building charges.

Your daughter, Mae, waits at home with the rent notice. She's twelve. She understands the arithmetic better than you do. She counted the deductions last night at the kitchen table, her finger tracing the column of numbers while the radiator hissed. "Three more clicks," she said, "and we're short for the month." She didn't cry. She calculated.

The cage drops. Second floor. Ladies' ready-to-wear. The floor-walker, Miss Chen, checks her wristwatch against the indicator. She times her lunch to your rhythm. If you're late at 4, she misses her sandwich. She docks your bonus. She smiles while she does it. "Building policy," she says. "Consistency standards."

You tap the glass. *Tap, tap.* The arrow settles.

First floor. Lobby. The superintendent stands by the marble directory, his ledger open. He doesn't write. He watches. The arrow between floors is a living thing now — you've seen it hesitate, seen it linger, seen it refuse to move until you find the exact pressure, the exact tap. Neglect makes it stubborn. Care makes it compliant. Rough handling makes it lie.

Mae's voice in your head: *Three more clicks.*

The cage rises. Empty. The ram shudders. Your knee clicks — once, twice, three times. The arrow trembles between 2 and 3. You tap. *Tap.* It holds. You tap harder. *Tap.* It drops to 2.

The superintendent's pencil moves.

You reach the fifth floor. Executive offices. Empty cage. The indicator reads 5. You tap anyway — *tap, tap, tap* — the rhythm your body knows. The arrow doesn't move. It doesn't need to. But your thumb remembers the glass, the cold brass, the price of neglect.

Mae's rent notice. The seventeen cents. The twenty-two. The three clicks remaining.

You press the emergency stop lever. The cage shudders, halts between floors. The superintendent's pencil stops. Somewhere below, a floor-walker checks her watch. A millinery buyer misses her sandwich. The building groans, settling deeper into its foundation.

Your knee clicks one last time.

You release the lever. The cage rises, smooth and silent, the arrow gliding to 5 without a tap.

The superintendent closes his ledger.

You ride down again, empty, leveling each floor perfect, each click of your knee a payment, each tap of your thumb a receipt. Mae will count them tonight. She'll know which ones you chose.
