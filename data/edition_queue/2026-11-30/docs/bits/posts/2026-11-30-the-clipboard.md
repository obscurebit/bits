# The Clipboard

The clipboard hit the Formica with a sound like a gavel. We watched the pen cap click, once, twice, a nervous tic against the damp chill of the probate office mudroom. Wet wool steamed off the heirs' shoulders. The floor wax smelled like lemon and old varnish. Outside, the November rain blurred the parking lot into a grey smear.

The Kitchen
The lawyer never showed. We knew he wouldn't. He was a voice on a speakerphone in a heated office downtown, billing four hundred an hour to tell us to initial page four. The property line dispute hinged on a fence post rotted to the dirt twenty years back. Our job—my job, our job—was to walk the perimeter with the measuring wheel, click-click-click, logging coordinates for a deed transfer nobody wanted to sign. The plumb bob in my canvas pouch swung heavy against my thigh, the braided nylon line frayed where I'd chewed it once, waiting for a witness who smoked too fast. The tobacco tin for the spare batteries was bent, the lid held by a rubber band. A mundane financial detail: the county paid thirty-two dollars a day for disinterested witnesses. The notary's cousin, Dave, and a process server named Ruiz split it. Dave picked at his cuticles. Ruiz read a folded racing form.

The Hallway
The queue for the single working pen had its own morality. You didn't jump. You didn't ask to borrow. You waited until the clipboard passed, warm from the previous hands. My sister, Elena, treated the logistics as proof of love. She drove the ninety minutes from the city, she found the parking spot, she brought the thermos of burnt coffee. She stood by the radiator, steam rising from her coat, checking her watch. *Did you get the initials on the easement page?* she asked, not looking at me. *Ruiz is on the phone with his bookie,* I said. *Dave lost the cap.* The pen cap clicked again. Click. Click. The measuring wheel in the truck bed had a flat spot. Thump-thump, thump-thump, every revolution a skipped beat.

The Mudroom
We would have measured the line true if the ground hadn't frozen. We would have signed the transfer if the heirs hadn't hated each other more than they wanted the money. The plumb bob would have hung straight if the wind hadn't gusted through the broken sash window, knocking the thermal curtains against the glass. The conditional perfect is a luxury of the warm. In the cold, you only have what you hold. The clipboard held the deed, the map, the carbon copies smudged grey. My fingers were stiff. Elena's gloves were leather, lined with cashmere. Mine were cotton, frayed at the fingertips. *Just initial it,* she whispered. *Make it done.* The queue shifted. A shoulder brushed mine. The air tasted of floor wax and wet dog.

The Door
Ruiz snapped his racing form shut. *I'm good,* he said. He initialed page four, page seven, page twelve. His handwriting was a jagged scrawl. Dave followed, meticulous, dotting every 'i'. The notary stamped, embossed, signed. The deed was a brick of paper. Elena held out her hand for the clipboard. *Give it here. I'll file it. You're done.* The measuring wheel thumped in the truck bed, a loose heart. The plumb bob swung in the pouch, a pendulum counting down. I looked at the initials. Ruiz. Dave. Me. The line on the map was a fiction we had walked into existence. The fence post was dust. The lawyer's voice crackled on the speakerphone, *Exhibit A accepted.* Elena's fingers closed on the clip. *Let go,* she said. *It's over.* I held the board. The pen cap clicked in my pocket. *No,* I said. *You file it.*
