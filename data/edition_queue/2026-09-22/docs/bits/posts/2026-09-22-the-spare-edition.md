# The Depot's Final Schedule

We knew the tram line died in '87, same year the council stopped fixing the roof. What we didn't expect was for the pigeons to keep running it.

Miguel's birds came back at dusk with transfers clipped to their legs. Not modern magnetic strips — the old cardboard punch tickets, blue for Zone 1, yellow for Zone 2, the kind conductors punched with that satisfying *chunk-chunk* sound. We watched from the break room, mugs steaming, as thirty birds landed on the rusted overhead lines and shed their cargo into the waiting trays Miguel had welded from bed frames.

"Route 44," Miguel read, squinting at a blue ticket under the bare bulb. "Final stop: Coppermill Bridge. 23:40 departure."

"Bridge been gone twenty years," said Toni, peeling an orange at the metal table. "Flood took it. Council never rebuilt."

"Tell the pigeons," Miguel said. He tucked the ticket into his logbook beside yesterday's haul: a Zone 3 red, two Zone 1 blues, a child's concession stamped with a cartoon fox. "They're punctual. More than the 44 ever was."

We laughed, the particular laugh of people who maintain things no one uses. The depot smelled of guano, damp concrete, and the specific grease Miguel swore by — rendered pork fat, his abuela's recipe, kept in a margarine tub. Three of us held keys. Four if you counted Miguel's niece, Elena, who held the deed.

She arrived at 6 AM with a clipboard and a buyer from a storage conglomerate. Navy suit, leather gloves, the smell of expensive hand sanitizer cutting through the depot's funk.

"Tomorrow," she told us, not Miguel. "Contracts signed. Demolition in six weeks."

Miguel didn't look up from his logbook. "The 23:40 from Coppermill arrives 00:15. That's tonight's schedule."

"There is no 23:40. There is no Coppermill. There is no schedule." Elena tapped her clipboard. "Uncle, this is real. They're turning this into climate-controlled units for people who own too many Christmas decorations."

"The birds don't know that."

"The birds are pests. The tickets are trash you've been collecting for — how long?"

"Since the line closed." He said it like she'd asked how long he'd been breathing.

We watched the silence stretch. Toni put down her orange. Dave, who'd driven the 44 for fifteen years before they cut it, shifted his weight.

"Elena," Dave said. "The tickets. They're dated. Tonight's batch — all stamped 2024."

She frowned. "Someone's printing fakes."

"Printing Zone 1 blues with the old punch pattern?" Miguel finally looked up. His hands were stained black at the fingertips. "The die broke in '89. I know. I tried to fix it."

The buyer cleared his throat. "Miss Varga, perhaps we —"

"Tomorrow," she repeated, softer now. "Sign the transfer papers. Or don't. The deed's in my name either way."

She left. The buyer followed, glancing at the pigeons roosting on the signal gantry.

We stood in the depot's dimness. The roof leaked onto Track 3. A distant train shook the walls.

"She'll come back," Toni said. "Needs someone to clear the birds before demolition."

"She won't sign," Miguel said. "Not tomorrow. Not ever."

"How do you know?"

He opened his logbook to a fresh page. The pigeons were already gathering on the overhead lines, silhouettes against the grimy windows. Dawn traffic hummed beyond the depot walls, real buses on real routes carrying real people to real jobs.

"Because tonight," Miguel said, picking up his pen, "the 23:40 brings something different. Always does, on the last run of the month."

We didn't ask what. We knew better. The depot held its breath. The pigeons rustled their wings. Somewhere in the city, a bridge that didn't exist anymore waited for a tram that would never come, carrying passengers who paid in cardboard and faith.

Miguel punched the date into the fresh page. *Chunk-chunk.*
